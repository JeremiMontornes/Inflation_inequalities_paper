\section{Aggregation Bias Across COICOP Digits}
\label{app:aggregation-bias}

Table~\ref{tab:aggregation-bias-excluding-rents-harmonized} compares inflation gaps across COICOP
digit depths using HBS microdata and reports a digit-3 benchmark from Eurostat national-quintile HBS aggregates.
Macro D3 excl. and all microdata columns exclude actual and imputed rents (HBS 041 and 042)
and the corresponding HICP actual-rent component (041), and use relative-expenditure weights.
All columns use annual-average chained-index inflation. Macro D3 excl. exactly reproduces the
excluding-rents gap in Table~\ref{tab:rent-mapping-robustness}.

\paragraph{Micro--macro consistency.}
We first reaggregate the 2020 household records into national income quintiles and compare
survey-weighted expenditure shares with Eurostat's published HBS shares on a common digit-3
support, excluding rents. Ranking households by total net household income substantially improves
this match relative to ranking them by equivalised income. Across products and quintiles, the mean
absolute difference in budget shares falls from 0.20 to 0.05 percentage points in the Netherlands
and from 0.31 to 0.04 in Belgium; the corresponding figures for France are 0.19 and 0.03.
This comparison precedes HICP calibration and therefore identifies a difference in the construction
of income groups rather than an effect of the inflation-index weighting method. We use total
household income for the microdata quintiles throughout this appendix.

\paragraph{Baskets and weights.}
Microdata quintiles are defined separately within each country using survey-weighted net household
income without division by an equivalence scale (total household income). The sample covers Germany, France, Spain,
the Netherlands and Belgium; Italy is excluded because household income is unavailable in its
microdata file. The 2020 microdata expenditure baskets are reconstructed at each digit depth from
the same finest available expenditure variables after removing rents. Nonpositive or missing
expenditure entries contribute zero, while eligible households remain in the survey denominators.

For product $j$ in quintile $q$, let $\bar{x}_{qj}$ be survey-weighted mean HBS expenditure and
$\bar{x}_j$ the corresponding national mean. The group weight in year $y$ is
\begin{equation}
 \omega_{qj,y}=
 \frac{W^{HICP}_{j,y}\,\bar{x}_{qj}/\bar{x}_j}
 {\sum_{k\in\mathcal J}W^{HICP}_{k,y}\,\bar{x}_{qk}/\bar{x}_k},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal J$ contains the matched non-rent products. Macro baskets use the corresponding
relative-expenditure information in the published Eurostat HBS tables, using the most recent available preceding HBS wave for each matched item. Each basket is normalised to one. RAS is not used in this table. Prices
are mapped to the HBS classification and reaggregated with annual HICP weights; rent components
are removed before constructing the broad housing index.

\paragraph{Inflation and aggregation bias.}
Let $I^d_{q,y,m}$ denote the chained Laspeyres index for quintile $q$ at digit depth $d$.
Inflation in 2022 is calculated from annual-average indices,
\begin{equation}
 \pi^d_{q,2022}=100\left(
 \frac{\frac{1}{12}\sum_{m=1}^{12} I^d_{q,2022,m}}
 {\frac{1}{12}\sum_{m=1}^{12} I^d_{q,2021,m}}-1\right).
\end{equation}
The gap is $G^d=\pi^d_{Q1,2022}-\pi^d_{Q5,2022}$ and the microdata aggregation bias is
$B^{d,d'}=G^d-G^{d'}$ for $d'>d$. Thus, negative bias indicates a larger gap at finer digit depth.

At digit 3, the micro--macro gap difference is below 0.1 percentage point in Germany, France,
Spain and Belgium, and about 0.3 point in the Netherlands. These residual differences should not
be interpreted as aggregation bias: the latter is measured within the microdata across digit
depths. The comparison with published HBS shares uses native digit-3 variables, whereas the
inflation calculations aggregate the finest available expenditure variables. In the Netherlands
and Belgium, these two constructions need not coincide, notably for insurance expenditure.
