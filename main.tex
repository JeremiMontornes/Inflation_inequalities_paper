\begin{abstract}
This article documents inflation inequality across household categories in the euro area using
harmonized HICP prices and household budget surveys. We construct household and category-level inflation indicators and assess recent fiscal
interventions using counterfactual price indices. Household-level inflation dispersion rises sharply
during the 2021--2023 episode. Household characteristics explain only 12.4\% of within-country variance on average across
months, leaving most dispersion within observationally similar household groups. The
inflation gap between the first and fifth income quintiles peaks at 1.2 percentage points in
2022 and is close to zero in 2021 and 2023.
Counterfactual indices indicate that household price policies, including tariff shields,
VAT cuts, fuel rebates, and price caps, have effects in 2022 of
$-1.5$ percentage points on annual-average inflation and $-0.6$ percentage point on the
Q1--Q5 inflation gap.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent \textbf{Keywords:} Inflation, Distribution of Income, Unconventional Fiscal Policy\\
\noindent \textbf{JEL codes:} E31, D30, C43

\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

This paper documents how the recent inflation surge affected euro-area households. Inflation
rates in the euro area rose sharply from mid-2021, displaying considerable dispersion
across countries and households. The 2021--2023 episode was
a large relative-price shock, initially driven by imported energy and commodity prices.

%incidence therefore mainly depended on household exposure to energy, including heating and

%The consumer price index (CPI) typically reflects an average rate of price change for an average household. However, households consume different baskets of goods, leading to wide variations around the average. The main objective of this paper is to document how households face heterogeneous inflation because of the differences in their consumption patterns, and to measure the effects of unconventional fiscal policies decided in 2022-2023.


Inflation inequality emerges when relative-price movements interact with heterogeneous household
expenditure shares. If all prices rose proportionally, fixed-basket inflation would be identical
across households. We examine how recent energy-price shocks and the fiscal response shaped household inflation
exposure across the euro area. Specifically, we construct monthly price indices at the household level and then separately for income, age, and area of residence groups in each euro-area country, following Eurostat's HICP methodology. We use individual 2020 HBS expenditure records both to construct household-specific indices and to aggregate expenditure into household-group baskets. The household-level indices show dispersion within groups as well as differences between group averages. We also construct counterfactual
price indices that remove the main price-based fiscal interventions of 2021--2023. This counterfactual is a direct consumer-price accounting exercise: it isolates the contribution of the measures to household inflation, holding expenditure weights fixed between scenarios and abstracting from behavioral responses.

Our main results are the following. Household-level dispersion rises sharply during the shock. Yet a model saturated with all interactions among income, age, household size, education, and residence explains only 12.4\% of within-country variance on average across months, ranging from 8.6\% to 17.4\%. The remaining 87.6\% lies within observationally similar household groups. Thus, income quintiles capture one systematic dimension of heterogeneity rather than the full distribution of household outcomes.
The Q1--Q5 inflation gap widens during the shock and varies across euro-area
countries. In 2022, the annual Quintile 1--Quintile 5 gap exceeds 3 points in Estonia,
Latvia, Lithuania, and the Netherlands and reaches 2.9 points in Ireland. In the euro area, the annual Q1--Q5 inflation gap is
0.1 point in 2021, 1.2 points in 2022, and 0.0 point in 2023 after rounding. Older and rural households experience higher inflation during the rise in energy prices.
Household price policies lower annual-average inflation by 0.1, 1.5, and 0.6 percentage points in
2021, 2022, and 2023, respectively. They narrow the annual Q1--Q5 inflation gap by 0.1, 0.6, and
0.5 percentage point in the same years.

A complementary input--output simulation assigns 6.0 percentage points of inflation exposure to the observed extra-EU energy import-price shock
and implies a 0.5-point widening of the Q1--Q5 income gap. Household energy makes a contribution of 1.6 points to this gap,
partly offset by transport and other products. Thus, households are
more exposed to the energy shock at the bottom of the income distribution, while the fiscal
response primarily cushions the household-energy channel.



The literature measures household inflation by combining price changes with differences in
expenditure patterns. For the United States, \citet{hobijn2005inflation} reweight aggregate price indices for demographic groups. More recently, \citet{jaravel2024distributional} constructs long-run distributional
consumer price indices for the United States, closely following the BLS methodology. Using household-level expenditure or scanner data,
\citet{kaplan2017inflation,argente2021cost} document household-level dispersion within broad income and demographic groups.
Using barcode-level grocery purchases in the United States, \citet{oflaherty2026lookwithin} shows that demographic group averages mask most household-level inflation dispersion.
In the euro area, \citet{ampudia2024shopping} show how household
shopping behavior shapes the effect of monetary policy on inflation heterogeneity along the income distribution.
\citet{pallotti2024bears} combine consumption, income, and balance-sheet data to
measure welfare losses across euro-area households. Building on \citet{charalampakis2022impact},
we take a complementary approach to \citet{oflaherty2026lookwithin}. Depending on the exercise, we
cover 17 to 20 euro-area countries and the full consumption basket, assigning a common country-product
HICP series to harmonized HBS expenditure categories. The policy counterfactual first reconstructs
component price paths at finer detail before mapping them to this common basket support. This broader scope permits harmonized cross-country
comparisons and monthly counterfactuals for price-based fiscal policies. Our RAS-calibrated baskets
also aggregate to official HICP item weights. The trade-off is that our measures capture heterogeneity
in expenditure shares, but not differences in prices paid, product quality, or substitution within
product categories \citep{sangani2026cheapflation}.

A related literature evaluates the fiscal response to the recent inflation surge. Using household microsimulation models,
 \citet{pallotti2024bears} and \citet{amores2025inflation} show that fiscal interventions reduced welfare losses and inequality,
although broad price measures were less targeted than income support. Using detailed UK household data,
\citet{levell2026welfare} highlight a trade-off between targeting and efficiency: energy price subsidies
better protect households with high exposure to energy-price shocks, including within income groups, but distort consumption choices,
while transfers preserve price signals but may be less well targeted.
For Germany, \citet{krebs2024pricecontrols} examine conditions under which energy price controls
can be optimal, while \citet{dertwinkelkalt2025energy} show that the design of the energy price brakes
can induce suppliers to raise prices while reinforcing households' incentives to save energy. \citet{bonnet2025compensating} show, for France, that income-targeted transfers are more effective than fuel price subsidies at limiting welfare losses relative to household income.
We instead combine harmonized household inflation measures with monthly counterfactual price paths reconstructed at the
COICOP-component level for all 20 euro-area countries. This approach identifies which products drove the distributional
effect of price-based policies: lower electricity and gas prices reduce the Quintile 1--Quintile 5 inflation gap,
while fuel measures partly offset that reduction because higher-income households spend more on private transport.

\section{Data and Methodology}

\label{sec:data-methodology}

Our approach constructs household and household-group price indices using the logic of
the Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices (HICP). We first describe the price and expenditure data,
sample coverage, product matching, and household groups. We then calibrate expenditure weights to
official HICP product weights, construct household and group indices, and aggregate the national
results to the euro area.

\subsection{Sources, Samples, and Product Coverage}

\subsubsection*{Sources and Samples}

The analysis combines public HICP data with restricted Household Budget Survey (HBS) microdata and
national HBS inputs. Headline HICP inflation measures price changes for the aggregate household
consumption basket. Each national HICP uses price changes for goods and services weighted by
household expenditure from national accounts, household budget surveys, and other national sources.
Eurostat then aggregates the national indices with country weights that reflect each country's share
of euro-area household consumption expenditure.\footnote{The calculation follows Regulation (EU)
2016/792 and common data-collection practices. HICP products follow the European Classification of
Individual Consumption according to Purpose (ECOICOP), and historical series are mapped to the
classification used in the HBS. National statistical institutes compile the HICP in collaboration
with Eurostat.}

Eurostat updates the HICP product weights annually. The new weights enter with the January release
and remain fixed within the calendar year. Let $\mathcal J_y$ denote the set of product categories
with official HICP weights in year $y$, $p_{j,y,m}$ the price index for product $j$ in month $m$, and
$p_{j,y,0}$ its price reference for the weight year, corresponding to the previous December. The
official within-year Laspeyres link and chained index are
\begin{align}
P_{y,m}
&=\sum_{j\in\mathcal J_y}\omega^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{j,y}
\frac{p_{j,y,m}}{p_{j,y,0}},\\
I_{y,m}&=I_{y-1,12}P_{y,m}.
\end{align}

Figure~\ref{fig:hicp} shows inflation across the main HICP components in the euro area since January
2021. Energy and fuel prices rise sharply from mid-2021 and peak in 2022. Inflation subsequently
declines across most components, with energy prices falling more sharply than food prices and other
components.

Our primary expenditure source is the 2020 HBS microdata. We use household expenditure records for
19 euro-area countries, excluding Italy. The records contain expenditure by product, total net
household income, and household characteristics. Group-level indicators cover all 20
euro-area countries through the national and tabulation-based exceptions described below. Household
distributions cover 152,613 households in 18 countries: Italy is excluded because its retained
national input supplies group baskets rather than household records, and Portugal because its
individual expenditure divisions cannot separate rents. These households remain fixed across price
years, so the monthly series does not represent a panel of newly observed consumption choices.
Figure~\ref{fig:hbs_monthly_inflation_percentiles} follows this sample from January 2021 to December
2025.

The 2020 HBS wave combines surveys collected over country-specific reference periods. Fieldwork dates
and survey frequency therefore need not coincide across countries. The fixed wave provides a
cross-section of consumption heterogeneity rather than annual observations on changing household
baskets.\footnote{See \citet{accardo2023measuring} on estimating changes in consumption inequality
between survey waves.} Repeated Spanish HBS waves suggest that broad differences in expenditure
shares between low- and high-income households remain relatively stable even when aggregate
consumption patterns change (Appendix~\ref{sec:app-additional-hbs-figures}).

\subsubsection*{Product Coverage and Treatment of Rents}

All HBS-based calculations in the main text use the three-digit ECOICOP1 classification and never
use finer household-expenditure detail. This is the
finest common household-level detail that retains France and Germany in the broad cross-country
sample. Depending on the country, this level represents roughly 45--50 product groups in the source
HBS; countries with finer expenditure codes are aggregated to this common support, and an unmatched
three-digit component is represented by its nearest common parent. Source price
series must be complete over the full calculation period before aggregation, which keeps the product
composition within each three-digit category fixed over time. The policy module can reconstruct
price interventions at four-digit detail, but these price paths are aggregated to the same
three-digit support before they are combined with HBS baskets.
Historical and validation exercises retain their explicitly stated matching levels.
Appendix~\ref{app:aggregation-bias} compares the measured Q1--Q5 inflation gap across two- to
five-digit COICOP levels and reports the matching level used in each specification.

The baseline excludes actual and imputed rents and removes the corresponding HICP components before
matching. Parent categories are then rebuilt, and the remaining HBS expenditure and HICP product
weights are normalized over the common non-rent support. This coverage applies to household indices,
group indices, and policy counterfactuals. The resulting measures capture non-rent consumption-price
exposure rather than an all-items cost-of-living index. Table~\ref{tab:rent-mapping-robustness}
reports alternative rent conventions, while Appendices~\ref{sec:app-weight-normalization}
and~\ref{sec:app-validation-tests} report the corresponding normalization and validation exercises.

\subsubsection*{Household Groups and National Exceptions}

We construct groups by income, age of the household reference person, and area of residence. The HBS
also records education, which enters the household-level variance decomposition. Residence groups use
the Degree of Urbanisation (DEGURBA).\footnote{DEGURBA uses a common European methodology based on a
1 km$^2$ population grid. It distinguishes cities (densely populated areas), towns and suburbs
(intermediate-density areas), and rural areas (thinly populated areas). Urban centres have at least
50,000 inhabitants and 1,500 inhabitants per km$^2$; urban clusters have at least 5,000 inhabitants
and 300 inhabitants per km$^2$; the remaining areas are rural.}

We use HBS survey weights throughout the construction of nationally representative group baskets.
Income groups rank households by total net household income without adjustment for household size.
They therefore describe household income rather than living standards per consumption unit. Evidence
from the United States shows why this distinction matters. Using Consumer Expenditure Survey data,
\citet{klick2024equivalized} find that equivalising income substantially changes the demographic
composition of income quintiles: the lowest quintile contains fewer one-person households and more
households with children. Yet the resulting changes in income-group inflation rates are small over
their 2006--2023 comparison period. Equivalisation therefore makes households within income groups
more comparable in terms of living standards without necessarily changing the qualitative inflation
gradient.
National income-quintile thresholds are defined separately within each national HBS sample and survey wave. Missing income excludes a household from income
baskets but not from age, residence, or unconditional analyses when the required variables are
available. Appendix~\ref{sec:app-income-definition} compares this definition with equivalised income
while holding household inflation rates, the sample, and weights fixed.

The main 2021--2023 analysis uses fixed 2020 HBS baskets. For each national group, we aggregate
individual product expenditure into a representative consumption basket. Three national exceptions
preserve broader group coverage. Italy uses the national group
baskets described in Appendix~\ref{sec:app-italy-hbs}. Portugal uses published group tabulations for
non-rent inflation because its individual expenditure divisions cannot isolate rents. Malta uses
published residence-area baskets and relative-expenditure weighting because the required groups and
RAS margins are unavailable.

Figure~\ref{fig:hbs} compares income-quintile baskets at the euro area level. For descriptive
completeness, the figure includes actual and imputed rents, although both are excluded from the
inflation indices because their treatment is not sufficiently harmonized across countries.
Lower-income households allocate larger expenditure shares to food and housing. Within housing,
household energy is retained in the inflation indices and is central to the analysis. Exposure to a
given price shock therefore depends on both these basket differences and the products affected by the
shock. Housing energy and private-transport expenditure are also higher in rural areas than in cities,
reflecting differences in heating needs and mobility constraints.

\subsection{Calibration and Price Indices}
\label{sec:household-inflation-rates}

\subsubsection*{RAS Calibration}

Unadjusted HBS expenditure shares do not generally aggregate to the annual HICP
product weights. We therefore use HBS expenditure to distribute each official product total across
households and groups. Row and Column Adjustment Scaling (RAS) preserves the relative HBS expenditure
profiles while imposing margins consistent with the HICP construction.

For household $i$ in country $c$, let $a_{ic}$ denote its survey weight and $E_{ic,y}$ its retained
expenditure on the matched non-rent product set $\mathcal J_{c,y}$. Let
$\omega^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{c,j,y}$ be the official weight of product $j$ in year $y$. We normalize the
household expenditure and HICP product margins to sum to one:
\[
s_{ic,y}=\frac{a_{ic}E_{ic,y}}{\sum_h a_{hc}E_{hc,y}},
\qquad
\bar\omega^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{c,j,y}
=\frac{\omega^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{c,j,y}}
{\sum_{k\in\mathcal J_{c,y}}\omega^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{c,k,y}},
\]
where $h$ indexes households in country $c$. RAS rescales the household--product expenditure matrix
to match both margins. Its calibrated cells $M_{j,ic,y}$ satisfy
\[
\sum_{j\in\mathcal J_{c,y}}M_{j,ic,y}=s_{ic,y},
\qquad
\sum_i M_{j,ic,y}=\bar\omega^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{c,j,y}.
\]
Normalizing each household's calibrated expenditure gives its product weights:
\begin{equation*}
\omega_{j,ic,y}=\frac{M_{j,ic,y}}{s_{ic,y}},
\qquad
\sum_{j\in\mathcal J_{c,y}}\omega_{j,ic,y}=1.
\label{eq:household-product-weight}
\end{equation*}
The expenditure-weighted average of household baskets therefore reproduces the normalized HICP
product shares on the matched support.

For a household group $g$, the calibration replaces the individual margin with the group's share
$s_{g,y}$ of total HBS expenditure. If $M_{j,g,y}$ is the calibrated product--group mass, the group
basket is $\omega_{j,g,y}=M_{j,g,y}/s_{g,y}$. It sums to one, and its expenditure-weighted average
across groups reproduces the normalized HICP product shares. Appendix~\ref{sec:app-weight-normalization}
details the RAS algorithm and the matching of group margins to HBS waves.

\subsubsection*{Household-Level Price Indices}

Let $p_{c,j,y,m}$ denote the product price index in month $m$ of year $y$. Product indices are first
expressed relative to the preceding December, aggregated with annual household weights, and then
chained to the preceding December's household index:\footnote{See Eurostat's \texttt{hicp} R
package, which implements the Eurostat HICP aggregation.}
\begin{align}
P_{ic,y,m}
&=\sum_{j\in\mathcal J_{c,y}}\omega_{j,ic,y}
\frac{p_{c,j,y,m}}{p_{c,j,y-1,12}},\\
I_{ic,y,m}&=I_{ic,y-1,12}P_{ic,y,m}.
\label{eq:household-within-year-price-index}
\end{align}

The household application uses fixed 2020 expenditure profiles, while annual HICP margins update
aggregate product composition.

\subsubsection*{Group-Level Price Indices}

For each income, age, or residence group, we aggregate the HBS expenditure records before applying
RAS. Group indices therefore need not equal averages of separately calibrated
household indices. Suppressing country subscripts, the within-year link and chained group index are
\begin{equation}
P_{g,y,m}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal J_y}\omega_{j,g,y}
\frac{p_{j,y,m}}{p_{j,y-1,12}},
\qquad
I_{g,y,m}=I_{g,y-1,12}P_{g,y,m}.
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal J_y$ is the matched non-rent product set. The baseline uses the 2020 HBS wave
throughout; historical exercises using successive waves retain their stated matching convention.

\subsection{National and Euro-Area Aggregation}

We use two aggregation procedures for two distinct objects. First, the distributional bands in
Figure~\ref{fig:hbs_monthly_inflation_percentiles} summarize national household-inflation
distributions. Within each country, we calculate the mean and the 10th, 20th, 80th,
and 90th empirical percentiles of household year-on-year inflation. We then average each national
statistic using Eurostat's annual HICP country expenditure weights. For a national statistic
$s_{c,y,m}$, the reported series is
\[
s_{y,m}=\sum_c\omega_{c,y}s_{c,y,m},
\qquad
\omega_{c,y}=\frac{W_{c,y}}{\sum_{k\in\mathcal C}W_{k,y}},
\]
where $W_{c,y}$ is the published all-items HICP country weight and $\mathcal C$ is the fixed country
sample. These bands average national percentile endpoints; they are not percentiles of a pooled
euro-area distribution. The country weights are not additionally adjusted for rents.

Second, aggregate household and group price indices require aggregation of December-relative price
links before chaining. Let $q_{c,y}$ denote the annual HICP country expenditure weight after removing
rent expenditure and normalizing over the included countries, so $\sum_c q_{c,y}=1$. The household
aggregate is
\begin{align}
P_{c,y,m}
&=\frac{\sum_i a_{ic}E_{ic,y}P_{ic,y,m}}{\sum_i a_{ic}E_{ic,y}},\\
P_{y,m}&=\sum_c q_{c,y}P_{c,y,m},
\qquad I_{y,m}=I_{y-1,12}P_{y,m}.
\end{align}
The same sequence applies to group indices using group-specific expenditure masses. Averaging
household inflation rates directly does not implement this annual chain aggregation. All baseline inflation
results exclude actual and imputed rents. Official headline series and the
explicitly identified rent-sensitivity and validation exercises retain their stated comparison
coverage.

\section{Main Results}
\label{sec:main-results}

This section proceeds in three steps. First, it documents the distribution of inflation across
households and assesses how much of that dispersion is associated with observable characteristics.
Second, it compares average inflation across income, age, and residence groups. Third, it traces the
euro-area Q1--Q5 gap to product contributions and cross-country differences during the 2021--2023
energy crisis.

\subsection{Household-Level Inflation Dispersion}

%Euro area results indicate small or large differences across categories. We also examine product contributions to inflation inequalities.



%In 2022, the contributions of different product categories to the inflation gap (between low and high-income households) tended to cancel each other out (e.g., energy prices vs. services). However, in 2023, rising food prices disproportionately affected low-income households, increasing the inflation gap (Figure 3).

Figure~\ref{fig:hbs_monthly_inflation_percentiles} shows country-weighted national means and
P20--P80 and P10--P90 ranges from January 2021 to December 2025. The mean rises from 0.9\% in
January 2021 to a peak of 11.9\% in October 2022. In that month, the weighted national P10 and P90
cutoffs are 8.0\% and 16.5\%, respectively. The inflation surge therefore coincides with greater
dispersion across household baskets within countries.

The band widths summarize within-country basket differences, averaged using annual country expenditure weights. Although the underlying household sample is fixed, the households located at each national cutoff can change from month to month. The bands therefore describe the cross-sectional distribution in each month rather than the inflation path of a fixed set of households at those cutoffs. Moreover, unlike the transaction data used by \citet{kaplan2017inflation}, our data do not capture differences in prices paid by households for the same product within a country.

\subsection{Group-Level Inflation Differentials}

Group-level inflation differentials are a common way to illustrate inflation inequality in official
statistics: several national statistical institutes regularly publish consumer price indices or
inflation rates for household categories. These indicators provide a summary of
systematic exposure differences. Observable characteristics explain only a limited share of household-level inflation dispersion,
although the distributions above show meaningful shifts in their locations and tails.
The distinction between individual dispersion and group averages is substantial: in October 2022,
the P20--P80 range of household inflation reached 5.3 percentage points, more than twice the
2.0-point gap between the mean inflation rates of the first and fifth income quintiles.

The inflation gap between households in the bottom and top 20 percent of their
national income distributions reached 1.2 percentage points in 2022, its highest annual value in our
series since 2000.\footnote{Appendix~\ref{sec:app-long-run-inflation} presents the corresponding
long-run price indices.} Figure~\ref{fig:ea20_short_run_income} traces the monthly dynamics behind this annual result. From
January 2021 through September 2021, the gap remained between $-0.25$ and $0.25$ percentage point in
about four out of five months, consistent with the low-inflation environment. It then rose from 0.1
percentage point in September 2021 to the October 2022 peak before narrowing as energy inflation
receded. This time profile is consistent with recent evidence from
\citet{bobasu2023impact}, although differences in weighting and country coverage may affect the
measured size of the gap.

\subsection{Product and Country Heterogeneity During the 2022--2023 Energy Shock}

%We observe heterogeneity in inflation inequalities across the euro area. Is there a link between inflation inequalities and inflation levels?

%\subsection*{Four different Patterns of Inflation Inequality}

The euro-area averages mask offsetting product contributions and large cross-country differences.
The Q1--Q5 inflation gap can be interpreted as a weighted covariance between relative differences
in expenditure shares and product-level inflation. A product contributes positively to the gap when
it is relatively more important in the Q1 basket and its price increases. Conversely, a negative
contribution means that the product raises Q5 inflation more than Q1 inflation; it does not necessarily
indicate a decline in its price. Table~\ref{tab:ea20-income-gap-product-contributions} reports the
product-level terms of this decomposition. In 2022, household energy accounts for 3.1 points of average
inflation and contributes 1.9 points to the 1.2-point gap; food adds 1.9 points to average inflation
and 0.7 point to the gap. These positive distributional contributions are partly offset by personal
transport, other transport, and other products, which contribute $-0.4$, $-0.3$, and $-0.5$ percentage points
to the gap. Higher-income households devote larger expenditure shares to personal transport, other
transport, and the broad ``other'' category. Because prices in other
transport and other products rise more slowly than those of energy and food, however, their negative
contributions offset only part of the energy- and food-driven Q1 disadvantage. In 2023, the positive
food contribution is again offset by other product groups, leaving a zero aggregate gap after
rounding.

\section{Contributions of Unconventional Fiscal Policies to Inflation Inequality}
\label{sec:fiscal-policies}


During the 2021--2023 energy crisis, euro-area governments used electricity and gas price caps,
VAT and energy-tax reductions, and fuel rebates to limit the increase in consumer prices
\citep{arregui2022targeted,dao2023unconventional,garciaMiralles2023support}. This section isolates
the direct contribution of these measures to average inflation and inflation inequality. Unlike
income transfers, they alter retail prices and therefore enter the household price indices directly.

\subsection{Constructing Counterfactual Price Indices}
\label{sec:no-policy-construction}

We compare observed, policy-inclusive retail prices with counterfactual price paths that remove the covered energy and food price measures. This comparison isolates the measures' effects on household inflation exposure by holding consumption quantities fixed and allowing only prices to differ between the ``with-policy'' and ``without-policy'' scenarios. It is therefore an accounting counterfactual rather than a behavioral one: it does not capture quantity adjustments, such as the observed decline in residential energy consumption during the crisis.
Income transfers and firm-only measures are excluded because they do not directly alter the covered
consumer prices.

We combine the Bruegel energy-measures dataset\footnote{https://www.bruegel.org/dataset/national-policies-shield-consumers-rising-energy-prices} with national sources to classify price measures, adding Spanish and Portuguese food-product VAT cuts. Table~\ref{tab:ea20-price-counterfactual-measures} in the appendix reports the inventory.

The available information determines how we construct each price path. First, direct reconstructions use published reference tariffs, statutory tax rates, or statutory unit rebates, with coverage and pass-through specified. Second, calibrations choose a measure-specific intensity parameter so that the geometric mean of monthly counterfactual-to-observed price ratios over the active policy months matches the assumed component-level ratio in the measure-level assumptions file. These targets summarize coverage and pass-through using the policy inventory and its cited national sources; they are assumptions rather than observed counterfactual prices. Third, conditional tariff or bill benchmarks apply statutory rules to a reference contract or representative bill when household contract shares or consumption quantities are unavailable. Their results depend on the chosen reference contract and consumption quantity. They also depend on assumed policy exposure. These three procedures classify the information used to construct each counterfactual price path; the subsections below instead organize measures by policy instrument. Each instrument subsection combines the general price adjustment with representative country implementations and their limitations.

Counterfactual component price paths are first constructed at the four-digit COICOP level by replacing
each observed HICP component index with its counterfactual counterpart; they are then aggregated to
the common HBS support described in Section~\ref{sec:data-methodology}. Let $p_{j,t}$
denote the observed HICP component index for product $j$ in month $t$, and let $\widetilde p_{j,t}$
denote the corresponding counterfactual index. Here $t$ indexes calendar months, corresponding to
the year--month pair $(y,m)$ used above. Let $r_{j,t}$ denote the observed retail price per unit
and $\widetilde r_{j,t}$ its counterfactual counterpart, expressed in the same monetary units.
For each simulated measure, we apply the implied price ratio to the observed
component index\footnote{When several measures apply to the same product in the same country and month, the R scripts combine their counterfactual-to-observed price ratios by adding their deviations from one to a baseline ratio of one.}:
\begin{equation}
\widetilde p_{j,t}
=
p_{j,t}
\frac{\widetilde r_{j,t}}{r_{j,t}} .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{VAT and Non-Fuel Excise-Tax Reductions.}
For VAT reductions, the observed retail price includes the temporary tax rate. We recover the counterfactual price by removing that rate and applying the standard rate:
\begin{equation}
\widetilde r_{j,t}
=
r_{j,t}
\frac{1+\tau^{0}_{j,t}}{1+\tau^{1}_{j,t}},
\end{equation}
where $\tau^{0}_{j,t}$ is the standard VAT rate and $\tau^{1}_{j,t}$ is the temporary rate. Non-fuel ad valorem excises follow the same ratio logic. Road-fuel excise reductions are classified with other fuel-price reductions and use the unit-price adjustment described below. Coverage and pass-through are calibrated where the statutory information is incomplete.
France's electricity-tax cuts and Spain's energy-tax reductions are reconstructed from the
corresponding statutory rates. Spanish fuel rebates are treated as unit rebates, as described below.

\begin{equation}
\widetilde r_{j,t}
=

\end{equation}
When the observed tariff and the counterfactual reference tariff are both available, the counterfactual retail
price is obtained by rescaling the observed price. If $T^{\mathrm{obs}}_{j,t}$ denotes the regulated
tariff actually applied and $T^{\mathrm{ref}}_{j,t}$ the tariff that would have applied without the
intervention, then:
\begin{equation}
r^{\mathrm{ref}}_{j,t}
=
r_{j,t}
\frac{T^{\mathrm{ref}}_{j,t}}{T^{\mathrm{obs}}_{j,t}} .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Subsidies, Rebates, and Other Fuel-Price Reductions.}
For unit subsidies and fuel rebates, we add the statutory absolute price wedge to the observed
monthly price path or, without a retail unit-price series, calibrate it in HICP component-index
units. Evidence from
\citet{drolsbach2023pass} suggests that temporary fuel-tax reductions and discounts were largely
passed through to consumer prices in 2022 in several euro-area countries, although pass-through
varied over time and by fuel type. This supports treating these measures as retail-price wedges in
the counterfactual construction.
Under full pass-through, adding the statutory support amount $s_{j,t}$ to the observed unit price gives:
\begin{equation}
\widetilde r_{j,t}
=
r_{j,t}+s_{j,t}.
\end{equation}
Germany's 2023 price brakes provided bill rebates on 80 percent of a predetermined reference quantity
\citep{krebs2024pricecontrols,dertwinkelkalt2025energy}. We use a representative-bill model anchored
on Bundesnetzagentur's April 2023 prices and infer the uncapped bill from monthly HICP movements under
fixed-quantity assumptions. This is a conditional benchmark because household contract shares and
consumption distributions are unavailable. Spain's transport-fuel rebates are reconstructed from the
statutory unit discounts.
For transport-fuel rebates, $s_{j,t}$ is recovered from the statutory pump discount, expressed in

rebates or subsidies because they reduced the tax-inclusive price paid by consumers, although their
technical implementation sometimes operated through fuel excise taxation rather than through a
direct transfer to households. The no-rebate fuel index is obtained by rescaling the observed HICP
transport-fuel component:
\[
\widetilde p_{j,t}
= p_{j,t}
\frac{r^{\mathrm{M}}_{j,t}+s_{j,t}}{r^{\mathrm{M}}_{j,t}} .
\]

equipment. The product decompositions nevertheless

unless they are affected by a separately identified measure.

We choose $\delta_k$ so that the geometric mean of this ratio over the active months equals the measure's assumed component-level price ratio.

\subsection{Euro-Area Effects on Inflation and Inflation Inequality}
\label{sec:temporal-fiscal-effects}

The price-based measures changed both the level and the timing of inflation during the energy
crisis. We isolate their direct effect
on consumer prices. Observed minus counterfactual euro-area annual inflation is $-0.1$ percentage point in 2021, $-1.5$ points in 2022, and $-0.6$ point in 2023 (Table~\ref{tab:country-heterogeneity-counterfactual-gap}). The 2022 estimate is
consistent in magnitude with \citet{dao2023unconventional}, who find that unconventional fiscal
policy measures reduced euro-area inflation by 1--2 percentage points in 2022 under different
modeling assumptions. Figure~\ref{fig:ea_policy_total_cpi_counterfactual} is constructed from the same observed and counterfactual product indices as Table~\ref{tab:country-heterogeneity-counterfactual-gap}; annual averaging of the plotted indices therefore reproduces the table estimates.
Counterfactual inflation lies above observed inflation during the main phase of the energy shock, with the
largest difference around the inflation peak in late 2022. VAT and non-fuel excise reductions, fuel-price reductions,
price shields, and regulated-price caps therefore compressed the peak.

A reduction in the price level need not lower inflation in every subsequent year. When a temporary measure expires or its price discount shrinks, observed prices rise relative to the lower comparison base created by the measure. Observed annual inflation can then exceed counterfactual inflation even if the observed price level remains no higher than its no-policy counterpart. Positive annual policy effects therefore can reflect the withdrawal of earlier support and base effects, rather than a higher price level caused by the policy. The same timing mechanism can reverse a policy's annual effect on the Q1--Q5 inflation gap.

\section{Household Inflation Exposure: Shocks and Burden}
\label{sec:household-level}

This section assesses direct and indirect exposure to extra-EU import-price shocks and measures the
inflation burden relative to household income.

\subsection{Direct and Indirect Exposure to Extra-EU Import-Price Shocks}

We apply the observed extra-EU import-price changes to imported final goods and intermediate inputs.
The FIGARO production network propagates the intermediate-input cost impulses across EA20 countries
and industries. We then map producer-price responses into consumption products and aggregate them
with the household expenditure weights used elsewhere in the paper. Throughout this subsection,
$k\in\{\text{energy},\text{food}\}$ indexes the import-price shock, $d$ denotes the destination
country, $i$ an imported product, $j$ a destination industry, and $g$ a household group. A
superscript $k$ labels the shock scenario; it is not an exponent.

Let $h_k$ denote the observed percentage change in the annual-average extra-EU import unit-value
index for shock $k$. For a final product $i$ absorbed by households in country $d$, let
$F_{di}^{\mathrm{extra}}$ denote imports from outside the EU27 and
$F_{di}^{\mathrm{all}}$ total household absorption, including domestic production and all imports.
The direct final-demand price impulse is
\begin{equation}
d_{di}^{k}=h_k\frac{F_{di}^{\mathrm{extra}}}{F_{di}^{\mathrm{all}}}.
\label{eq:direct-import-price-impulse}
\end{equation}
The ratio therefore scales the common external price shock by the extra-EU share of the product's
household supply. The vector $\mathbf d^k$ stacks $d_{di}^{k}$ over all country--product pairs.

For intermediate inputs, $\mathbf A$ is the FIGARO matrix of input coefficients for the EA20
country--industry network: each element records the amount of an input required per unit of gross
output in the purchasing industry. The vector $\mathbf b^k$ contains the first-round production-cost
impulses generated by extra-EU inputs affected by shock $k$. Its element for industry $j$ in country
$d$ is
\begin{equation}
b_{dj}^{k}
=h_k\frac{\sum_{o\notin EU27}\sum_{i\in\mathcal S_k}Z_{oi,dj}}{x_{dj}},
\label{eq:main-import-cost-impulse}
\end{equation}
where $o$ indexes the origin country, $\mathcal S_k$ is the set of products affected by shock $k$,
$Z_{oi,dj}$ is the FIGARO intermediate flow of product $i$ from origin $o$ to industry $j$ in
destination country $d$, and $x_{dj}$ is that industry's gross output. Thus, $b_{dj}^{k}$ is the
first-round increase in unit production costs under complete pass-through of the import-price shock.
The vector $\mathbf b^k$ stacks these impulses over all EA20 country--industry pairs.

The corresponding producer-price response is
\begin{equation}
\Delta\mathbf p^{k}=(\mathbf I-\mathbf A^{\mathsf T})^{-1}\mathbf b^{k},
\label{eq:leontief-import-price-response}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf I$ is the identity matrix and $(\mathbf I-\mathbf A^{\mathsf T})^{-1}$ is the
Leontief price inverse. The vector $\Delta\mathbf p^k$ includes both the initial imported-input cost
impulse and all subsequent rounds of cost propagation through domestic and intra-EA20 input links.

Finally, $\mathbf C$ is the normalized NACE--COICOP concordance matrix: multiplying an
industry-level price vector by $\mathbf C$ yields the corresponding vector of COICOP
consumption-price changes. The
vector $\mathbf w_g$ contains the normalized COICOP expenditure shares of household group $g$, so
its elements sum to one. The total inflation exposure of group $g$ is
\begin{equation}
\Delta\pi_g^k
=\mathbf w_g^{\mathsf T}\mathbf C\mathbf d^{k}
+\mathbf w_g^{\mathsf T}\mathbf C\Delta\mathbf p^{k}.
\label{eq:import-shock-household-effect}
\end{equation}
The first term is the contribution of directly imported final goods; the second is the contribution
of imported intermediate inputs after production-network propagation. Because $h_k$ and all price
responses are expressed as percentage changes, $\Delta\pi_g^k$ is an inflation exposure in percentage
points. The effect on inflation inequality is
$\Delta\pi_{Q1-Q5}^k=\Delta\pi_{Q1}^k-\Delta\pi_{Q5}^k$, where a positive value means that shock $k$ raises
the inflation exposure of the first income quintile more than that of the fifth.

Panel (a) uses the observed increase in the annual-average extra-EU energy import unit-value index
from 2021 to 2022, and panel (b) uses the corresponding food import-price increase. The raw shocks,
their conversion into country-industry cost impulses, and the propagation equations are reported in
Appendix~\ref{sec:app-import-cost-impulses}. The shocks are common external-price innovations, while
bilateral import shares, production structures, and household baskets generate heterogeneous
country and household exposure.

\subsection{Inflation Burden}

Our indicators measure differences in inflation exposure across household groups, but they do
not directly express the cost of inflation relative to household resources. We therefore construct
the burden from HBS microdata rather than combining group-level inflation with an external aggregate
consumption-to-income ratio. For household $i$ in country $c$, let $X_{ic}$ denote consumption
expenditure on the non-rent products matched to HICP, $Y_{ic}$ net household income, and
$\bar{\pi}_{ic,y}$ the household's average annual inflation rate in year $y$, computed with the
RAS-calibrated weights described in Section~\ref{sec:household-inflation-rates}. The raw
household-level burden is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{raw}}_{ic,y}=\bar{\pi}_{ic,y}\frac{X_{ic}}{Y_{ic}},
\label{eq:micro-inflation-burden}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{\pi}_{ic,y}$ is measured in percent, so $\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{raw}}_{ic,y}$ is measured
in points of net income. The indicator allows both individual inflation exposure and the
consumption-to-income ratio to vary across households. The expenditure numerator and inflation
index cover the same matched non-rent products. This fixed-resource exposure indicator is not
compensating variation: it holds 2020 expenditure and income fixed and allows neither substitution
nor income adjustment.

Very small reported incomes can generate mechanically extreme expenditure-to-income ratios. We
therefore winsorize $100X_{ic}/Y_{ic}$ at the survey-weighted 99th percentile within each country,
while retaining the underlying household inflation rate. Denoting the resulting ratio by
$\widetilde{\kappa}_{ic}$, the burden used in the estimates and its aggregation for national income
quintile $q$ are
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{B}_{ic,y}=\bar{\pi}_{ic,y}\frac{\widetilde{\kappa}_{ic}}{100},
\qquad
\mathcal{B}_{q,y}=\frac{\sum_{c}\sum_{i\in q(c)}a_{ic}\mathcal{B}_{ic,y}}
{\sum_{c}\sum_{i\in q(c)}a_{ic}},
\label{eq:aggregate-micro-inflation-burden}
\end{equation}
where $a_{ic}$ is the HBS survey weight and $q(c)$ indicates that total-net-income quintiles are
defined separately within each country before households are pooled. The survey weights expand the
microdata to national household populations and therefore determine the euro-area aggregation.
The 2020 HBS basket, income, and expenditure observations are held fixed, while household-specific
inflation is calculated for 2021--2023. For each household-year, the inflation rate is computed over
the matched non-rent expenditure categories and the HBS--HICP product weights are renormalized to
one. We impose no minimum matched-expenditure threshold.

Net household income enters this calculation twice: it determines the national income quintile and
appears in the denominator of $X_{ic}/Y_{ic}$. The Q1--Q5 gradient can therefore arise partly from
the mechanical expenditure-to-income gradient, even if households face the same inflation rate.
We report the estimates as a descriptive fixed-resource exposure measure and do not interpret the
quintile difference as the causal effect of heterogeneous inflation. Separating the inflation and
expenditure-to-income components requires a common-inflation counterfactual and sensitivity checks
using medians, alternative winsorization thresholds, and equivalised income.

Higher-income households also generally have greater capacity to absorb a price shock by reducing
discretionary expenditure or substituting toward cheaper varieties. Appendix
Figure~\ref{fig:app-ea-essentials-discretionary} documents the corresponding difference in basket
composition.

Figure~\ref{fig:ea20-inflation-burden} uses household indices and matched non-rent expenditure.
The first quintile faces a burden of 2.5 percent of net income in 2021, 8.7 percent in 2022, and
4.8 percent in 2023, compared with 1.5, 4.7, and 3.0 percent for the fifth quintile. The Q1--Q5 gap
is 1.0, 4.0, and 1.8 points, respectively, calculated before rounding. The income and expenditure
filters retain positive-income households; national quintiles are assigned before these
burden-specific exclusions.

\section{Conclusion}


%This paper a set of public indicators to measure inflation inequalities in real-time monthly
%across socio-demographic groups in the Euro Area.

%Inflation inequality during the 2021--2023 episode varied more across countries, age groups, and
%residence areas than across income quintiles in the euro-area aggregate.

Household expenditure baskets reveal differences in exposure to the recent inflation surge. Modest income-quintile gaps at the euro-area level conceal larger differences within several countries. Older and rural households also experience higher inflation during the rise in energy prices, although these associations vary over time.

The fixed-resource burden measure is higher relative to net income for households in the lowest
income quintile than for those in the highest. This descriptive gradient combines differences in
household inflation with the mechanically higher expenditure-to-income ratios of low-income
households and should not be interpreted as a pure inflation-exposure effect.

The price-policy counterfactual indicates that household price measures lower average inflation during the energy crisis and narrow the gap between the lowest and highest income quintiles. Relief on household energy prices accounts for most of the narrowing, while motor-fuel measures partly offset it. These accounting effects depend on the reconstructed and calibrated price wedges and do not measure the policies' full welfare or macroeconomic effects.

A model saturated with all interactions among observed household characteristics explains only
12.4\% of household inflation variance on average. The remaining 87.6\% lies within observationally
similar groups. The main distributional gradients are therefore informative about systematic
exposure, but they summarize only a limited part of household inflation dispersion. This pattern
partly reflects aggregation: a group-average basket spreads expenditure across the many products
purchased by its members and therefore mechanically attenuates extreme exposures, whereas an
individual household concentrates expenditure on a much narrower set of products. Uneven product
price changes can consequently generate substantial idiosyncratic inflation even within narrowly
defined demographic groups \citep{oflaherty2026grocery}.

\section{Country Exposure to Extra-EU Import-Price Shocks}
\label{sec:app-import-cost-impulses}

For shock family $k$, let $h_k$ denote the observed change in the annual-average extra-EU27 import
unit-value index. The energy shock is 83.9 percent and the food shock is 21.0 percent between 2021
and 2022. These are common external-price innovations: country heterogeneity enters through import
exposure rather than through different values of $h_k$.

For destination country $d$ and industry $j$, the raw import-price shock becomes the imported-input
cost impulse
\begin{equation}
b_{dj}^{k}
=h_k\frac{\sum_{o\notin EU27}\sum_{i\in\mathcal S_k}Z_{oi,dj}}{x_{dj}},
\label{eq:appendix-import-cost-impulse}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal S_k$ is the set of shocked products, $Z_{oi,dj}$ is the intermediate-input flow from
origin $o$ and product $i$ to industry $j$ in country $d$, and $x_{dj}$ is that industry's gross
output. Thus, an identical raw price shock produces different cost impulses across countries and
industries because their extra-EU imported-input shares differ.

The output-weighted country impulse reported in
Table~\ref{tab:extra-eu-import-cost-impulses-country-2022} is
\begin{equation}
\bar b_d^k
=h_k\frac{\sum_j\sum_{o\notin EU27}\sum_{i\in\mathcal S_k}Z_{oi,dj}}
{\sum_j x_{dj}}.
\end{equation}

\section{The Household Budget Survey in Italy}
\label{sec:app-italy-hbs}

The most recent complete harmonized Eurostat HBS entry for Italy is 2005. We therefore combine it
with the 2015 and 2020 Istat public-use HBS files to construct the household-group baskets.

Age-group baskets are computed directly from the survey-weighted expenditure aggregates in the
Istat files, using the age of the household reference person.

For income quintiles, households are ranked by equivalised total consumption using the Carbonaro
scale and the survey weights. Because income quintiles are not observed in the target-year files, we
transport the relationship between income- and expenditure-quintile baskets observed in 2005. Let
$E_{jqy}$ denote expenditure on COICOP group $j$ by expenditure quintile $q$ in year $y$, and let
$s^{I}_{jq,2005}$ and $s^{E}_{jq,2005}$ denote the corresponding 2005 shares for income and
expenditure quintiles. The estimated income-quintile consumption structure is

\begin{equation}
\widehat{s}^{I}_{jqy}
=
\frac{E_{jqy}\left(s^{I}_{jq,2005}/s^{E}_{jq,2005}\right)}
{\sum_k E_{kqy}\left(s^{I}_{kq,2005}/s^{E}_{kq,2005}\right)}.
\label{eq:italy-income-expenditure-bridge}
\end{equation}

Thus, the 2005 income-to-expenditure ratio adjusts each 2015 or 2020 expenditure-quintile component,
after which the basket is renormalized to one. A few broad groups are combined to reconcile the two
2005 classifications. The resulting income-quintile structures are estimated rather than directly
observed. The procedure addresses the same
absence of jointly observed income and consumption discussed by \citet{donatiello2022joint}.

Table~\ref{tab:it-hbs-all-products} reports the resulting shares of total consumption. Age shares are
direct survey estimates; income-quintile shares are bridge estimates. The first and fifth income
quintiles account for about 8 and 39 percent of Italian consumption, respectively.

\section{RAS Weighting}
\label{sec:app-weight-normalization}

We use the RAS (Row and Column Adjustment Scaling) algorithm. RAS is a bi-proportional matrix
scaling method that alternately rescales rows and columns until the required marginal totals are
matched \citep{bacharach1965estimating}. In our setting, we combine official HICP item weights with
the relative HBS expenditure profile of each household group.

The baseline removes actual and imputed rents (HBS 041 and 042 and corresponding HICP
rent components) before constructing matched product baskets. Parent housing aggregates
are recalculated without these components. RAS matches group expenditure margins and annual
HICP product margins normalized over the remaining matched non-rent products.
The resulting indices therefore measure inflation excluding rents; agreement with the
all-items HICP is not an identity implied by this calibration.

Rent treatment can affect both the magnitude and the sign of income gaps because housing
tenure and rent shares vary across groups \citep{geerolf2023inflation}.
Table~\ref{tab:rent-mapping-robustness} retains three alternative conventions as a separate
sensitivity comparison: all rents maps HBS 041+042 to HICP 041; effective rents retains
HBS 041 only; excluding rents removes 041 and 042 and renormalizes the remaining basket.
This reference exercise uses relative-expenditure weights. It is distinct from the
RAS-based baseline and the RAS micro--macro comparisons in Appendix~\ref{app:aggregation-bias};
its excluding-rents column need not reproduce their estimates.

\input{tables/tab_EA20_rent_mapping_robustness_2021_06_2023_06}


We distinguish three objects: the HICP product shares, the aggregate product-by-group expenditure
mass calibrated by RAS, and the resulting within-group baskets. First, let $s_{g,y}$ denote the
share of group $g$ in total consumption expenditure used for HICP weight year $y$:
\begin{equation}
s_{g,y}
=
\frac{E^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{g,y'}}{E^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{\mathrm{all},y'}},
\qquad
\sum_g s_{g,y}=1,
\end{equation}
where $y'$ is the HBS wave matched to HICP weight year $y$. In the baseline, $y'=2020$ and the expenditure margins are computed from the same household sample as the group baskets. Historical applications select the most recent available preceding HBS wave, or the earliest wave when no preceding observation exists. These targets are expenditure shares, not population shares. For Italy, the income targets use the Istat-based HBS
inputs described in Appendix~\ref{sec:app-italy-hbs}.

Let $\mathcal{J}_y$ be the set of matched COICOP categories available in the HICP weighting
structure in year $y$. Let $E^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{j,g,y'}$ denote expenditure on item $j$ by group $g$ in the
matched HBS wave $y'$, and let $E^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{j,\mathrm{all},y'}$ denote expenditure on that item by all
households. Consistent with the implementation, the HBS correction factor used in the RAS seed is
the share of aggregate expenditure on item $j$ accounted for by group $g$:
\begin{equation}
c^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{j,g,y'}
=
\frac{E^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{j,g,y'}}{E^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{j,\mathrm{all},y'}}.
\end{equation}
Thus $c^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{j,g,y'}$ records how expenditure on item $j$ is distributed across household groups.
To avoid mixing index-weight units with expenditure shares, define the HICP weight normalized over
the matched product set as
\begin{equation}
\bar{\omega}^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{j,y}
=
\frac{\omega^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{j,y}}
{\sum_{k \in \mathcal{J}_y}\omega^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{k,y}},
\qquad
\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_y}\bar{\omega}^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{j,y}=1.
\end{equation}
The initial product-by-group mass is then
\begin{equation}
Z_{j,g,y}
=
\max\left\{
s_{g,y}\bar{\omega}^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{j,y}c^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{j,g,y'},\epsilon
\right\},
\qquad
\epsilon=10^{-12}.
\end{equation}
The small positive floor is a numerical safeguard to keep the RAS problem strictly positive
after HICP-HBS matching. RAS transforms this seed into a joint expenditure-share matrix
$M_{j,g,y}$. Its group margins equal the groups' expenditure shares, while its product margins equal
the normalized HICP shares:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_y} M_{j,g,y}
=
s_{g,y}
\quad \text{for all } g,
\qquad
\sum_g M_{j,g,y}
=
\bar{\omega}^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{j,y}
\quad \text{for all } j.
\end{equation}
Both sets of margins sum to one, so the constraints are mutually compatible.

The RAS algorithm starts from $M^{(0)}_{j,g,y}=Z_{j,g,y}$ and repeats two scaling steps. At
iteration $t$, the group-margin adjustment is
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{M}^{(t)}_{j,g,y}
=
M^{(t-1)}_{j,g,y}
\times
\frac{
s_{g,y}
}{
\sum_{\ell \in \mathcal{J}_y} M^{(t-1)}_{\ell,g,y}
},
\end{equation}
and the product-margin adjustment is
\begin{equation}
M^{(t)}_{j,g,y}
=
\widetilde{M}^{(t)}_{j,g,y}
\times
\frac{
\bar{\omega}^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{j,y}
}{
\sum_h \widetilde{M}^{(t)}_{j,h,y}
}.
\end{equation}
We iterate these two steps by weight year until the maximum absolute row or column marginal error is
below $10^{-10}$, with a maximum of 1,000 iterations.

The calibrated matrix $M$ is a joint expenditure distribution. The group-specific HICP basket is
the corresponding conditional product distribution within group $g$:
\begin{equation}
\omega_{j,g,y}
=
\frac{M_{j,g,y}}{s_{g,y}}
=
\frac{M_{j,g,y}}{\sum_{k \in \mathcal{J}_y}M_{k,g,y}}.
\end{equation}
The equality of the two denominators follows directly from the group-margin constraint. It implies
that each group basket sums to one. Moreover, the expenditure-share-weighted average of the group
baskets reproduces the normalized HICP product weights:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_y}\omega_{j,g,y}=1,
\qquad
\sum_g s_{g,y}\omega_{j,g,y}
=
\bar{\omega}^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{j,y}.
\end{equation}

When reported as percentages, the group-specific weights are $100\omega_{j,g,y}$ and therefore sum
to 100 within every household group. This rescaling leaves the RAS constraints and the resulting
price indices unchanged.

This preserves the relative information contained in the HBS expenditure profiles while making the
aggregate of the household-group baskets consistent with the published HICP weighting structure, up
to residual differences induced by product coverage and COICOP matching.
