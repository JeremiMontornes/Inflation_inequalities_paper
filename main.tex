\begin{abstract}
This article documents inflation inequality across household categories in the euro area using
harmonized HICP prices and household budget surveys. We construct  household and category-level inflation indicators and assess recent fiscal
interventions using counterfactual price indices. During the 2021--2023 inflation episode, the
annual inflation gap between the first and fifth income quintiles peaks at 1.2 percentage points in
2022 and is close to zero in 2021 and 2023.
Counterfactual indices indicate that household price policies, including tariff shields,
VAT cuts, fuel rebates, and price caps, lower annual inflation by about 0.7 percentage point
on average over 2021--2023 and reduce the annual income-quintile inflation gap by 0.4 percentage point on
average.


\vspace{1em}

\noindent \textbf{Keywords:} Inflation, Distribution of Income, Unconventional Fiscal Policy\\
\noindent \textbf{JEL codes:} E31, D30, C43

\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

This paper examines the effect of the recent inflation surge on euro-area households. Inflation
rates in the euro area rose sharply from mid-2021 onward, displaying considerable dispersion
across countries and socio-demographic groups. Aggregate inflation masks differences in household exposure because expenditure shares vary
across income, age, and residence groups. High inflation disproportionately affects households whose
consumption baskets are more exposed to rapidly rising prices, in particular households with higher
expenditure shares on energy and food. The 2021--2023 episode was
a large relative-price shock, initially driven by imported energy and commodity prices.

%Its
%incidence therefore mainly depended on household exposure to energy, including heating and
%transport \citep{charalampakis2022impact,gautier2024effects,corsello2026inflation}.

%The consumer price index (CPI) typically reflects an average rate of price change for an average household. However, households consume different baskets of goods, leading to wide variations around the average. The main objective of this paper is to document how households face heterogeneous inflation because of the differences in their consumption patterns, and to measure the effects of unconventional fiscal policies decided in 2022-2023.


Inflation inequality requires both relative-price changes and heterogeneous
expenditure shares. If all prices rose proportionally, fixed-basket inflation would be identical
across households. Inequality emerges when relative-price movements interact with differences in
household consumption baskets. We examine how recent energy-price shocks and the fiscal response shaped household inflation
exposure across the euro area. Specifically, we construct monthly price indices by income, age, and residence
area for each euro-area country and aggregate them using Eurostat's HICP methodology. The consumption baskets are individual 2020 HBS expenditure records, from which we construct both household-group baskets and household-specific indices. Published group tabulations and the Italian national approximation are used only where the required microdata are unavailable. We use the household indices to measure dispersion within and across groups and construct counterfactual
price indices that remove the main price-based fiscal interventions of 2021--2023. This counterfactual is a direct consumer-price accounting exercise: it isolates the contribution of the covered measures to household inflation, holding expenditure weights fixed between scenarios and abstracting from behavioral responses and general-equilibrium effects.

Our main results are the following. Inflation inequality varies substantially across euro-area
countries and years. In 2022, the annual Quintile 1--Quintile 5 gap exceeds 3 points in Estonia,
Latvia, Lithuania, and the Netherlands and reaches 2.9 points in Ireland. In the euro area, the annual income gap is
0.1 point in 2021, 1.2 points in 2022, and 0.0 point in 2023 after rounding. In regressions pooling 2021--2023, the
average annual age and rural--city gaps are about 0.6 and 0.4 points. The inflation burden is 2.5,
8.7, and 4.8 percent of net income for the
first quintile in 2021, 2022, and 2023, compared with 1.5, 4.7, and 3.0 percent for the fifth
quintile. Older and rural households experience higher inflation during the rise in energy prices.
In household-level regressions, income, age, and residence are associated with exposure during the energy
shock. The rural--urban gap is large in 2021--2022, whereas the age
gradient remains visible in 2022--2023; the association with income varies over time. Finally, household price
policies lower annual inflation by about 0.7 percentage point on average over 2021--2023 and reduce the
annual income-quintile gap by about 0.4 percentage point on average. A complementary input--output simulation shows
that the observed extra-EU energy import-price shock generates 6.5 points of inflation exposure
and widens the Q1--Q5 gap by 1.1 points. Household energy contributes 1.7 points to this gap,
partly offset by vehicle operation and other products. Thus, households remain
more exposed to the energy shock at the bottom of the income distribution, while the fiscal
response primarily cushions the household-energy channel.



The literature measures household inflation by combining price changes with differences in
expenditure patterns. Early contributions reweight aggregate price indices for demographic groups
\citep{hobijn2005inflation} in the United States. More recently, \citet{jaravel2024distributional} constructs long-run distributional
consumer price indices for the United States, closely following the BLS methodology. Using household-level expenditure or scanner data,
\citet{kaplan2017inflation,argente2021cost} document substantial dispersion within those groups.
In the euro area, \citet{ampudia2024shopping} show how household
shopping behavior shapes the effect of monetary policy on inflation heterogeneity along the income distribution.
\citet{pallotti2024bears} combine consumption, income, and balance-sheet data to
measure welfare losses across euro-area households. Building on \citet{charalampakis2022impact},
we construct monthly indices by income, age, and residence area for all 20
euro-area countries. Unlike existing cross-country indicators, our RAS-calibrated baskets aggregate
to official HICP item weights. Because we assign a common price index to all households within each
COICOP category, our measures capture expenditure-share heterogeneity but not differences in prices
paid, product quality, or within-category substitution \citep{sangani2026cheapflation}.

A related literature evaluates the fiscal response to the recent inflation surge. Using household microsimulation models,
 \citet{pallotti2024bears} and \citet{amores2025inflation} show that fiscal interventions reduced welfare losses and inequality,
although broad price measures were less targeted than income support. Using detailed UK household data,
\citet{levell2026welfare} highlight a trade-off between targeting and efficiency: energy price subsidies
better protect households with high exposure to energy-price shocks, including within income groups, but distort consumption choices,
while transfers preserve price signals but may be less well targeted.
For Germany, \citet{krebs2024pricecontrols} examine conditions under which energy price controls
can be optimal, while \citet{dertwinkelkalt2025energy} show that the design of the energy price brakes
can induce suppliers to raise prices while reinforcing households' incentives to save energy. \citet{bonnet2025compensating} show, for France, that income-targeted transfers are more effective than fuel price subsidies at limiting welfare losses relative to household income.
We instead reconstruct monthly counterfactual price paths at the
COICOP-component level for all 20 euro-area countries. This approach identifies which products drove the distributional
effect of price-based policies: lower electricity and gas prices reduce the Quintile 1--Quintile 5 inflation gap,
while fuel measures partly offset that reduction because higher-income households spend more on private transport. Our contribution is to combine harmonized household inflation measures with a product-level
decomposition of price-policy effects across 20 euro-area countries.

\section{Data and Methodology}

\label{sec:data-methodology}

Our approach constructs price indices for households and household groups using the
same accounting logic as the Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices (HICP). We describe the data
and harmonization challenges, then calibrate expenditure weights to official HICP weights.
% Moreover, we provide an open-access package that enables users to replicate and update our indicators.

\subsection{Calculating Household Inflation Rates}
\label{sec:household-inflation-rates}

%The aim of calculating inflation  by household category is to construct inflation  that takes account of differences in household consumption structure. The data includes HICP by country, HBS weights (with a discussion on harmonization), and country weights based on country consumption.


Headline HICP inflation measures price changes for the aggregate household consumption basket.
We construct corresponding indices for income groups, age groups, residence areas, and individual
households. Where microdata are available, household groups aggregate individual expenditure records using HBS survey weights; their consumption baskets therefore reflect the expenditure of their constituent households. 
Prices are common within each national product category, as in the official HICP, while
expenditure weights vary across groups or households. We first construct individual household indices. Household-group indices provide an intermediate level of aggregation; the final step summarizes inflation across the euro area population and distinguishes the average household rate from inflation in aggregate expenditure.

%The exceptions needed to retain 20-country group coverage are set out below.

%The measurement problem is
%therefore to construct group-specific baskets that preserve observed household expenditure
%heterogeneity while remaining consistent with the published HICP aggregation.

This analysis combines public HICP data with restricted HBS microdata and national HBS inputs. The HICP is constructed by national statistical
 institutes in collaboration with Eurostat. The HICP
offers a standardized measure of inflation, ensuring comparability across Member States.  Each country's HICP is based on price changes for a fixed basket of goods
and services, weighted according to household expenditure from country-specific sources, including
national accounts, household budget surveys, and other national sources. The national indices are
then aggregated into the euro-area HICP using country-specific weights that reflect their share of
total household consumption expenditure.\footnote{The calculation ensures comparability by adhering to
Regulation (EU) 2016/792 and consistent data collection practices. The HICP is compiled according to
the European Classification of Individual Consumption according to Purpose (ECOICOP). Historical series are mapped to the classification used in the HBS.}

To ensure the HICP reflects recent consumption patterns, Eurostat updates its weighting structure
annually. The updated weights are implemented with the January release of the HICP and remain fixed
throughout the calendar year, which is the basis for the within-year Laspeyres
aggregation. Appendix~\ref{app:aggregation-bias} examines how the measured Q1--Q5 inflation gap
changes across COICOP digit depths 2 to 5, with a 3-digit benchmark. Let $\mathcal{J}_y$ denote
the set of product categories with official HICP weights in year $y$. Let $p_{j,y,m}$ denote the
price index of product category $j$ in month $m$ of year $y$, and let $p_{j,y,0}$ denote the price
reference for the HICP weight year, corresponding to the previous December. The official within-year
Laspeyres index is obtained by aggregating unchained product indices with the annual HICP product
weights:

\begin{equation}
P_{y,m}
=
\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_y}
\omega^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{j,y}
\cdot
\frac{p_{j,y,m}}{p_{j,y,0}}.
\end{equation}

The chained HICP index is then obtained by linking this within-year index to the previous December:

\begin{equation}
I_{y,m}
=
I_{y-1,12}
\cdot
P_{y,m}.
\end{equation}



Figure~\ref{fig:hicp} shows inflation developments across the main HICP components in the euro area since January 2021. The
series show marked volatility in energy and fuel prices. The sharp rise starting
around mid-2021 and peaking in 2022 reflects the post-COVID recovery, supply-chain disruptions, and
geopolitical tensions. After the 2022 peaks, inflation declined across most components. Energy prices
dropped sharply, while food prices and other components stabilized more gradually.

\subsection*{Consumption Data and Harmonization Challenges}

Our primary consumption source is the 2020 HBS household microdata. We harmonize household expenditure records for 19 euro-area countries, excluding Italy. These records contain expenditure by product, survey weights, total net household income, and household characteristics. They serve two purposes: survey-weighted aggregation produces the baskets used for income, age, and residence groups, while the underlying records identify household-specific inflation and its dispersion. Group baskets are therefore constructed from the individual observations wherever the required detail is available, rather than taken from published Eurostat HBS tabulations.

The available inputs support different country samples. Group-level indicators cover all 20 euro-area countries through the explicitly documented national and tabulation-based exceptions below. The household distributions cover 152,613 households in 18 countries: Italy is excluded because the retained national input supplies group baskets rather than household records, and Portugal because its individual expenditure divisions cannot separate rents. These households are held fixed across price years; the monthly series is not a panel of newly observed consumption choices. Figure~\ref{fig:hbs_monthly_inflation_percentiles} follows this sample from January 2021 to December 2025, and the paired policy-effect distribution uses 2021--2023.

Income-based estimates require finite household income and a positive reported equivalence scale, without dividing income by that scale. Complete-case regression restrictions and the positive-income filter for the burden are documented below. Historical and external-validation exercises retain their documented survey waves and income definitions.

%Cross-country comparisons require harmonization of national survey designs and product classifications.

HBS expenditure records combine survey information collected over country-specific reference periods and classified by COICOP product category. The harmonized wave used here is labelled 2020; fieldwork dates and national survey frequency need not coincide across countries. The fixed wave provides a cross-section of consumption heterogeneity rather than annual observations on changing household baskets.\footnote{See \citet{accardo2023measuring} on estimating developments in consumption inequality between survey waves.}

National statistical institutes in Italy and Spain also provide higher-frequency HBS data that can be used for country-specific extensions.
Repeated HBS waves available for Spain suggest that, while
consumption patterns can change substantially over a few years, differences
in the expenditure shares of the main consumption categories between low- and high-income households remain
relatively stable (see Appendix~\ref{sec:app-additional-hbs-figures}). Changes in
overall consumption patterns may therefore have a limited effect on measured inflation inequality
when consumption shares move in the same direction across income groups.

While HBS expenditure shares are observed only at low frequency, our weighting approach combines
household-specific HBS expenditure structures with monthly HICP inflation rates. Thus,
the aggregate weighting structure changes with annual HICP margins, while household expenditure
profiles remain fixed between survey observations.

Figure~\ref{fig:hbs} presents consumption baskets by income quintile. Lower-income households allocate
a larger share of expenditure to essentials such as food and energy, increasing their exposure to
price shocks in these categories. Housing and food account for a substantially larger
share of expenditure among lower-income households. Energy-related expenditure for housing and
private transport is also much higher in rural areas than in cities, reflecting differences in
heating needs and mobility constraints.


HICP and HBS are not directly harmonized. HICP item weights are updated every year and may rely on
national accounts or other national sources, while HBS expenditure structures are observed at lower
frequency. Depending on the level of data available, HICP and HBS are therefore merged at the three-digit
COICOP in each country. In some cases, data published directly by national statistical institutes
can be used instead of relying only on the Eurostat dataset. This approach is particularly valuable
when the nationally published data provide greater granularity, recency, or frequency.

The HBS contains many household characteristics that can be used to segment inflation exposure (income, education, age of the household reference person, and area of residence).\footnote{In recent HBS waves, Eurostat generally requests the Degree of
Urbanisation (DEGURBA) variable, built from a common European methodology based on a 1 km$^2$
population grid. It classifies residence areas into cities (densely populated areas), towns and
suburbs (intermediate density areas), and rural areas (thinly populated areas), using comparable
thresholds across EU countries: urban centers of at least 50,000 inhabitants and 1,500 inhabitants
per km$^2$, urban clusters of at least 5,000 inhabitants and 300 inhabitants per km$^2$, and all
remaining areas.} Our microdata income groups rank households by total net household income, without adjustment for household size. This choice differs from ranking households by equivalised income; it describes income at the household level rather than living standards per consumption unit. Appendix~\ref{sec:app-income-definition} compares the two income definitions while holding household inflation rates, the sample, and weights fixed. The effect on the measured Q1--Q5 inflation gap varies across countries: equivalisation narrows the gap in some countries and widens it in others. Published fallback tabulations retain their source classifications, and the Italian income groups are estimated as described below. Our baseline excludes actual and imputed rents and the corresponding
HICP rent components before matching, reaggregating parent categories and normalizing weights.
The remaining HBS baskets and HICP product shares are normalized over the matched non-rent
products. This common coverage applies to household- and group-level indices and policy counterfactuals.
It measures non-rent consumption-price exposure rather than an
all-items cost-of-living index. Appendix~\ref{sec:app-weight-normalization} describes the
calibration; Table~\ref{tab:rent-mapping-robustness} retains alternative rent conventions as a
separate sensitivity exercise. 

The main 2021--2023 analysis and the short-run figures use fixed 2020 HBS baskets.
For each national group, we sum individual product expenditure using survey weights and divide
by the group's weighted household population. The RAS expenditure margins use the same households
and expenditure coverage. Income quintiles use survey-weighted national thresholds of total net
household income, without equivalisation. Missing income excludes a household from income baskets,
but not from age, residence, or unconditional analyses when the required information is available.

These data constraints motivate the weighting methodology below. A simple use of HBS expenditure
shares would generally not aggregate back to the official HICP, because the HBS all-household basket
does not necessarily coincide with the annually updated HICP item weights. We therefore start from
the official HICP product weights and use HBS to distribute each product's expenditure across
households and, at the intermediate aggregation level, household groups. The Row and Column Adjustment Scaling (RAS) calibration preserves the relative HBS
expenditure profiles while imposing the row and column margins required by the HICP construction.


\subsection*{Household-Level Price Indices}

For household $i$ in country $c$, let $a_{ic}$ denote its survey weight and $E_{ic}$
its expenditure on the matched non-rent product set $\mathcal J_{c,y}$. Let
$\omega^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{c,j,y}$ be the official weight of product $j$ in year $y$.
We normalize the household expenditure margins and HICP product margins to sum to one:
\[
s_{ic,y}=\frac{a_{ic}E_{ic}}{\sum_h a_{hc}E_{hc}},
\qquad
\bar\omega^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{c,j,y}
=\frac{\omega^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{c,j,y}}
{\sum_{k\in\mathcal J_{c,y}}\omega^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{c,k,y}},
\]
where $h$ indexes households in country $c$. RAS rescales the household--product
expenditure matrix to match both margins. Its calibrated cells $M_{j,ic,y}$ satisfy
\[
\sum_{j\in\mathcal J_{c,y}}M_{j,ic,y}=s_{ic,y},
\qquad
\sum_i M_{j,ic,y}=\bar\omega^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{c,j,y}.
\]
Normalizing each household's calibrated expenditure gives its product weights:
\begin{equation}
\omega_{j,ic,y}=\frac{M_{j,ic,y}}{s_{ic,y}},
\qquad
\sum_{j\in\mathcal J_{c,y}}\omega_{j,ic,y}=1.
\label{eq:household-product-weight}
\end{equation}
Thus, expenditure-weighted household baskets reproduce the HICP product shares on
matched support. Appendix~\ref{sec:app-weight-normalization} details the RAS algorithm.

Product indices are first expressed relative to the preceding December, aggregated
with annual household weights, and then chained to the previous December's household
index.\footnote{See Eurostat's \texttt{hicp} R package, which implements the Eurostat HICP aggregation.}
Let $p_{c,j,y,m}$ denote the product price index in month $m$ of year $y$.

\subsection{Aggregating Household Inflation Rates}
\subsection*{Group-level Price Indices}

For each income, age, or residence group $g$, we aggregate expenditure records using survey
weights before applying RAS. Group indices therefore need not equal averages of separately
calibrated household indices. Country subscripts are suppressed below.

\paragraph{Group-Specific Weights.}
The calibration follows the household procedure, replacing individual expenditure margins
with the group's share $s_{g,y}$ of total HBS expenditure. If $M_{j,g,y}$ is the calibrated
product--group mass, the group basket is $\omega_{j,g,y}=M_{j,g,y}/s_{g,y}$. It sums to one,
and its expenditure-weighted average across groups reproduces the normalized HICP product
shares. Appendix~\ref{sec:app-weight-normalization} gives the group margins and their
matching to HBS waves. The baseline uses the 2020 wave throughout.

\paragraph{Group-Level Consumer Price Indices.}
Using the same December-relative aggregation and chaining as for households, we obtain
\begin{equation}
P_{g,y,m}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal J_y}\omega_{j,g,y}
\frac{p_{j,y,m}}{p_{j,y-1,12}},
\qquad
I_{g,y,m}=I_{g,y-1,12}P_{g,y,m},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal J_y$ is the matched non-rent product set, $P_{g,y,m}$ the within-year
price link, and $I_{g,y,m}$ the chained group index. Group calculations can use
parent-category prices when detailed series are missing; household calculations retain
usable prices and weights at the required depth. Calibration targets the matched non-rent
basket; the all-items reference exercises are reported in
Appendices~\ref{sec:app-weight-normalization} and~\ref{sec:app-validation-tests}.

\subsection*{Mean Household Inflation and Aggregate Inflation}

Figure~\ref{fig:hbs_monthly_inflation_percentiles} uses a two-stage aggregation. Within each country, we calculate the mean and the 10th, 20th, 80th and 90th empirical percentiles of household year-on-year inflation using HBS survey weights. We then average each national statistic across countries using Eurostat's annual HICP country expenditure weights. For a national statistic $s_{c,y,m}$, the reported series is
\[
s_{y,m}=\sum_c \omega_{c,y}s_{c,y,m}, \qquad
\omega_{c,y}=\frac{W_{c,y}}{\sum_{k\in\mathcal C}W_{k,y}},
\]
where $W_{c,y}$ is the published all-items HICP country weight and $\mathcal C$ is the fixed country sample. The bands therefore average national percentile endpoints; they are not percentiles of a pooled euro-area distribution. These weights are not additionally adjusted for rents. Averaging these statistics is distinct from constructing aggregate HICP inflation.

TLet $q_{c,y}$ be the annual HICP country expenditure weight, adjusted to exclude rents. We aggregate weights before chaining:
\begin{align}
L_{c,y,m}&=\frac{\sum_i a_{ic}E_{ic}L_{ic,y,m}}{\sum_i a_{ic}E_{ic}},\\
L_{y,m}&=\sum_c q_{c,y}L_{c,y,m},
\qquad I_{y,m}=I_{y-1,12}L_{y,m}.
\end{align}
Averaging household inflation rates directly does not implement this annual chain aggregation.

Official headline series and the explicitly identified rent-sensitivity and validation
exercises retain their stated comparison coverage.

\subsection{Heterogeneous Effects of the 2022--2023 Energy Shock Across Countries}

The 2021--2023 inflation episode was caused by a large energy-price shock, especially in natural gas markets,
following Russia's invasion of Ukraine. Although this shock was common to the euro area, its
distributional effects differed across countries because energy mixes, import dependence, household
consumption patterns, and fiscal responses were not uniform.

Table~\ref{tab:country-heterogeneity-cumulative-gap} documents this cross-country heterogeneity by
reporting annual-average inflation and the annual Quintile 1--Quintile 5 inflation gap. The gap is
largest in 2022 in most countries: it exceeds 3 points in Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, and the Netherlands and reaches
2.9 points in Ireland. Several countries subsequently record a negative gap in 2023 as energy-price
pressures recede, while the euro-area gap falls from 1.2 points in 2022 to zero after rounding in 2023. This
cross-country pattern is qualitatively similar to \citet{claeys2022inflation}, who also find larger
inflation gaps between low- and high-income households in countries with higher inflation.

group-specific expenditure shares explains these differences. A price increase in a product category generates inflation inequality only to
the extent that the affected category has different expenditure weights across household groups.
Hence, similar income-quintile consumption profiles can still produce different inflation gaps when
national energy prices, food prices, or fiscal cushioning measures evolve differently. Conversely,
large aggregate shocks may generate limited inequality when price increases fall on categories with
similar weights across quintiles or when policy measures dampen prices in essential energy
components.

\section{Effects of Household Fiscal Policies on Inflation Inequality}
\label{sec:fiscal-policies}

This section evaluates the distributional effects of unconventional fiscal policies implemented during the 2021--2023 energy crisis
that directly changed consumer prices faced by households, including electricity and gas price caps and shields,
VAT and energy-tax reductions and fuel rebates.

When a supply shock such as that of 2021--2023 threatens to make inflation persistent, central banks tighten monetary policy to keep inflation expectations anchored and prevent second-round effects. A conventional fiscal response would reinforce this adjustment by tightening the budget to cool aggregate demand and, ultimately, inflation, at the cost of lower output in the short run. Unconventional fiscal measures, such as energy subsidies and tax reductions, can instead support households and businesses while directly lowering the prices they pay and temporarily reducing measured inflation \citep{dao2023unconventional}. Targeting support at low-income households can also cushion the unequal burden of the shock, but these interventions entail a fiscal cost and increase public debt unless offset by other budgetary measures. Price-based support can affect measured inflation immediately upon implementation, whereas monetary policy works through longer and variable transmission lags: ECB model estimates place the peak effect on inflation in the second year, making roughly 18 months an indicative horizon rather than a fixed delay \citep{lane2022transmission}. These differences in timing and distributional incidence motivate our analysis of household price policies.

\subsection{Constructing Counterfactual Price Indices}
\label{sec:no-policy-construction}

In response to the energy crisis, euro-area governments introduced a broad array of measures to
mitigate household exposure to rising energy costs
\citep{arregui2022targeted,dao2023unconventional,garciaMiralles2023support}.
We compare observed policy-inclusive retail prices with counterfactual price paths that remove the covered energy and food price measures. This is a direct consumer-price accounting exercise: expenditure weights are fixed between scenarios, and we do not model changes in consumption, firms' pricing responses outside the covered components, or feedback through demand. The resulting differences measure the direct contribution of these measures to inflation and inflation inequality, rather than their welfare effects.

We combine the Bruegel's energy measures dataset\footnote{https://www.bruegel.org/dataset/national-policies-shield-consumers-rising-energy-prices} with national sources to identify price measures, including Spanish and Portuguese food-product VAT cuts. Table~\ref{tab:ea20-price-counterfactual-measures} in the appendix reports the inventory and identifies the simulated measures. The fiscal accounting assigns a documented cost to each of the 191 measures active in the counterfactual during 2021--2023 and counts shared envelopes once. Some envelopes cannot be split by instrument or targeting status, and some include business beneficiaries, so these costs do not isolate amounts received by households.

The available information determines how we construct each price path. First, direct reconstructions use published reference tariffs, statutory tax rates, or statutory unit rebates, with coverage and pass-through specified. Second, calibrations choose a measure-specific intensity parameter so that the geometric mean of monthly counterfactual-to-observed price ratios over the active policy months matches the assumed component-level ratio in the measure-level assumptions file. These targets summarize coverage and pass-through using the policy inventory and its cited national sources; they are assumptions rather than observed counterfactual prices. Third, conditional tariff or bill benchmarks apply statutory rules to a reference contract or representative bill when household contract shares or consumption quantities are unavailable. Their results depend on the stated contract, quantity, and exposure assumptions. The following country examples illustrate these differences before we set out the price adjustments by instrument.

Euro-area countries differed in their choice of instruments, combining tax reductions, price controls, energy-bill rebates, and direct income transfers in different proportions. France froze regulated gas tariffs at their October 2021 level through 2022, then limited their increase in early 2023, while also limiting increases in regulated electricity tariffs and cutting electricity taxes. Germany's 2023 household gas and electricity price brakes instead provided bill rebates linked to the positive difference between contractual and guaranteed prices on 80 percent of a predetermined reference quantity, preserving the contractual price at the margin \citep{krebs2024pricecontrols,dertwinkelkalt2025energy}. The Netherlands capped household gas and electricity prices in 2023 up to uniform annual consumption limits; when binding, the cap lowered the marginal price within the covered block, whereas the German rebate was based on a predetermined reference quantity \citep{haan2023energy}. Spain combined energy-tax cuts and fuel rebates with limits on regulated gas-tariff increases and the Iberian mechanism, jointly implemented with Portugal, which compensated eligible fossil-fuel generators to lower wholesale electricity prices, with the compensation financed by a levy on benefiting buyers and congestion revenues. These differences in policy design and coverage matter for both household exposure and the construction of counterfactual prices.

For Germany's price brakes, we use a representative-bill model anchored on Bundesnetzagentur's April 2023 prices and the statutory rebate on 80 percent of reference consumption, with monthly HICP movements used to infer the uncapped bill under fixed quantity assumptions; this is a conditional model, not an observed counterfactual tariff series.
For Slovenia's electricity and gas caps, we use a published-contract
benchmark. We add the positive difference between the GEN-I July 2022 new-customer supply
tariff and the statutory household cap, including the VAT applicable that month, to a full-bill
price anchored on SURS fourth-quarter 2022 averages and scaled by the monthly product HICP.
Network charges and excise are held fixed in this cap calculation. The legal cap window runs
from September 2022 through December 2023; the benchmark gas tariff falls below the cap
in November 2023, so its cap effect is then zero. This calculation assumes full exposure to the
reference contract. National contract shares are unavailable, and the resulting effect remains
a conditional benchmark, with alternative contract and exposure scenarios reported separately.
The cap calculation retains the contemporaneous VAT rate; it does not itself reverse the
separate VAT reduction. The Slovenian inventory is not exhaustive. Enterprise-only support
is excluded from the household inventory. The counterfactual assumptions table
reports the executed measures and their information basis.

Counterfactual price indices are constructed at COICOP digit 3 by replacing each observed
HICP component index with its counterfactual counterpart. Let $p_{j,t}$
denote the observed HICP component index for product $j$ in month $t$, and let $\widetilde p_{j,t}$
denote the corresponding counterfactual index. Here $t$ indexes calendar months, corresponding to
the year--month pair $(y,m)$ used above. Let $r_{j,t}$ denote the observed retail price per unit
and $\widetilde r_{j,t}$ its counterfactual counterpart, expressed in the same monetary units.
For each simulated measure, we apply the implied price ratio to the observed
component index\footnote{When several measures apply to the same product in the same country and month, the R scripts combine their counterfactual-to-observed price ratios by adding their deviations from one to a baseline ratio of one.}:
\begin{equation}
\widetilde p_{j,t}
=
p_{j,t}
\frac{\widetilde r_{j,t}}{r_{j,t}} .
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}

=
r_{j,t}
\frac{1+\tau^{0}_{j,t}}{1+\tau^{1}_{j,t}},
\end{equation}

The price-based measures changed both the level and the timing of inflation during the energy
crisis. We isolate their direct effect
on consumer prices. Observed minus counterfactual euro-area annual inflation is $-0.1$ percentage point in 2021, $-1.5$ points in 2022, and $-0.6$ point in 2023 (Table~\ref{tab:country-heterogeneity-counterfactual-gap}). The 2022 estimate is
consistent in magnitude with \citet{dao2023unconventional}, who find that unconventional fiscal
policy measures reduced euro-area inflation by 1--2 percentage points in 2022 under different
modeling assumptions. Figure~\ref{fig:ea_policy_total_cpi_counterfactual} is constructed from the same observed and counterfactual product indices as Table~\ref{tab:country-heterogeneity-counterfactual-gap}; annual averaging of the plotted indices therefore reproduces the table estimates.
Counterfactual inflation lies above observed inflation during the main phase of the energy shock, with the
largest difference around the inflation peak in late 2022. VAT and excise reductions, fuel rebates,
price shields, and regulated-price caps therefore compressed the peak.

A reduction in the price level need not lower inflation in every subsequent year. When a temporary measure expires or its price discount shrinks, observed prices rise relative to the lower comparison base created by the measure. Observed annual inflation can then exceed counterfactual inflation even if the observed price level remains no higher than its no-policy counterpart. Positive annual policy effects therefore can reflect the withdrawal of earlier support and base effects, rather than a higher price level caused by the policy. The same timing mechanism can reverse a policy's annual effect on the Q1--Q5 inflation gap.

Income and education coefficients are no longer statistically significant at 10 percent, but the age gradient remains: inflation is 0.5 percentage point lower for households with reference persons below 30 than for those aged 60 or over, significant at 1 percent. \subsection{Direct and Indirect Exposure to Extra-EU Import-Price Shocks}

We apply the observed extra-EU import-price changes to imported final goods and intermediate inputs.
The FIGARO production network propagates the intermediate-input cost impulses across EA20 countries
and industries. We then map producer-price responses into consumption products and aggregate them
with the household expenditure weights used elsewhere in the paper. Throughout this subsection,
$k\in\{\text{energy},\text{food}\}$ indexes the import-price shock, $d$ denotes the destination
country, $i$ an imported product, $j$ a destination industry, and $g$ a household group. A
superscript $k$ labels the shock scenario; it is not an exponent.

Let $h_k$ denote the observed percentage change in the annual-average extra-EU import unit-value
index for shock $k$. For a final product $i$ absorbed by households in country $d$, let
$F_{di}^{\mathrm{extra}}$ denote imports from outside the EU27 and
$F_{di}^{\mathrm{all}}$ total household absorption, including domestic production and all imports.
The direct final-demand price impulse is
\begin{equation}
d_{di}^{k}=h_k\frac{F_{di}^{\mathrm{extra}}}{F_{di}^{\mathrm{all}}}.
\label{eq:direct-import-price-impulse}
\end{equation}
The ratio therefore scales the common external price shock by the extra-EU share of the product's
household supply. The vector $\mathbf d^k$ stacks $d_{di}^{k}$ over all country--product pairs.

For intermediate inputs, $\mathbf A$ is the FIGARO matrix of input coefficients for the EA20
country--industry network: each element records the amount of an input required per unit of gross
output in the purchasing industry. The vector $\mathbf b^k$ contains the first-round production-cost
impulses generated by extra-EU inputs affected by shock $k$. Its element for industry $j$ in country
$d$ is
\begin{equation}
b_{dj}^{k}
=h_k\frac{\sum_{o\notin EU27}\sum_{i\in\mathcal S_k}Z_{oi,dj}}{x_{dj}},
\label{eq:main-import-cost-impulse}
\end{equation}
where $o$ indexes the origin country, $\mathcal S_k$ is the set of products affected by shock $k$,
$Z_{oi,dj}$ is the FIGARO intermediate flow of product $i$ from origin $o$ to industry $j$ in
destination country $d$, and $x_{dj}$ is that industry's gross output. Thus, $b_{dj}^{k}$ is the
first-round increase in unit production costs under complete pass-through of the import-price shock.
The vector $\mathbf b^k$ stacks these impulses over all EA20 country--industry pairs.

The corresponding producer-price response is
\begin{equation}
\Delta\mathbf p^{k}=(\mathbf I-\mathbf A^{\mathsf T})^{-1}\mathbf b^{k},
\label{eq:leontief-import-price-response}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf I$ is the identity matrix and $(\mathbf I-\mathbf A^{\mathsf T})^{-1}$ is the
Leontief price inverse. The vector $\Delta\mathbf p^k$ includes both the initial imported-input cost
impulse and all subsequent rounds of cost propagation through domestic and intra-EA20 input links.

Finally, $\mathbf C$ is the normalized NACE--COICOP concordance matrix: multiplying an
industry-level price vector by $\mathbf C$ yields the corresponding vector of COICOP
consumption-price changes. The
vector $\mathbf w_g$ contains the normalized COICOP expenditure shares of household group $g$, so
its elements sum to one. The total inflation exposure of group $g$ is
\begin{equation}
\Delta\pi_g^k
=\mathbf w_g^{\mathsf T}\mathbf C\mathbf d^{k}
+\mathbf w_g^{\mathsf T}\mathbf C\Delta\mathbf p^{k}.
\label{eq:import-shock-household-effect}
\end{equation}
The first term is the contribution of directly imported final goods; the second is the contribution
of imported intermediate inputs after production-network propagation. Because $h_k$ and all price
responses are expressed as percentage changes, $\Delta\pi_g^k$ is an inflation exposure in percentage
points. The effect on inflation inequality is
$\Delta\pi_{Q1-Q5}^k=\Delta\pi_{Q1}^k-\Delta\pi_{Q5}^k$, where a positive value means that shock $k$ raises
the inflation exposure of the first income quintile more than that of the fifth.

Panel (a) uses the observed increase in the annual-average extra-EU energy import unit-value index
from 2021 to 2022, and panel (b) uses the corresponding food import-price increase. The raw shocks,
their conversion into country-industry cost impulses, and the propagation equations are reported in
Appendix~\ref{sec:app-import-cost-impulses}. The shocks are common external-price innovations, while
bilateral import shares, production structures, and household baskets generate heterogeneous
country and household exposure.

\subsection{Inflation Burden}

Our indicators measure differences in inflation exposure across household groups, but they do
not directly express the cost of inflation relative to household resources. We therefore construct
the burden from HBS microdata rather than combining group-level inflation with an external aggregate
consumption-to-income ratio. For household $i$ in country $c$, let $X_{ic}$ denote consumption expenditure on the non-rent products matched to HICP, $Y_{ic}$ net household income, and $\bar{\pi}_{ic,y}$ the household's average annual
inflation rate in year $y$, computed with the RAS-calibrated weights described in Section~\ref{sec:household-inflation-rates}. The raw household-level burden is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{raw}}_{ic,y}=\bar{\pi}_{ic,y}\frac{X_{ic}}{Y_{ic}},
\label{eq:micro-inflation-burden}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{\pi}_{ic,y}$ is measured in percent, so $\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{raw}}_{ic,y}$ is measured in
points of net income. Thus, the indicator allows both individual inflation exposure and
the consumption-to-income ratio to vary across households. The expenditure numerator and inflation index cover the same matched non-rent products. This fixed-resource exposure indicator is not compensating variation: it holds 2020 expenditure and income fixed and allows neither substitution nor income adjustment.

Very small reported incomes can generate mechanically extreme expenditure-to-income ratios. We
therefore winsorize $100X_{ic}/Y_{ic}$ at the survey-weighted 99th percentile within each country,
while retaining the underlying household inflation rate. Denoting the resulting ratio by
$\widetilde{\kappa}_{ic}$, the burden used in the estimates and its aggregation for national income
quintile $q$ are
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{B}_{ic,y}=\bar{\pi}_{ic,y}\frac{\widetilde{\kappa}_{ic}}{100},
\qquad
\mathcal{B}_{q,y}=\frac{\sum_{c}\sum_{i\in q(c)}a_{ic}\mathcal{B}_{ic,y}}
{\sum_{c}\sum_{i\in q(c)}a_{ic}},
\label{eq:aggregate-micro-inflation-burden}
\end{equation}
where $a_{ic}$ is the HBS survey weight and $q(c)$ indicates that total-net-income quintiles are
defined separately within each country before households are pooled. The survey weights expand the
microdata to national household populations and therefore determine the euro-area aggregation.
The 2020 HBS basket, income, and expenditure observations are held fixed, while household-specific
inflation is calculated for 2021--2023. For each household-year, the inflation rate is computed over
the matched non-rent expenditure categories and the HBS--HICP product weights are renormalized
to one. We impose no minimum matched-expenditure threshold.

Higher-income households also generally have greater capacity to absorb a price
shock by reducing discretionary expenditure or substituting toward cheaper varieties. Appendix
Figure~\ref{fig:app-ea-essentials-discretionary} documents the corresponding difference in basket
composition.

Figure~\ref{fig:ea20-inflation-burden} uses household indices and matched non-rent expenditure. \section{Conclusion}


%This paper a set of public indicators to measure inflation inequalities in real-time monthly
%across socio-demographic groups in the Euro Area.

%Inflation inequality during the 2021--2023 episode varied more across countries, age groups, and
%residence areas than across income quintiles in the euro-area aggregate.

Household expenditure baskets reveal substantial differences in exposure to the recent inflation surge. Modest income-quintile gaps at the euro-area level conceal larger differences within several countries. Older and rural households also experience higher inflation during the rise in energy prices, although these associations vary over time.

Differences in household resources amplify the burden of inflation. Our fixed-resource exposure measure is higher relative to net income for households in the lowest income quintile than for those in the highest. Similar inflation rates can therefore impose different burdens relative to income.

The price-policy counterfactual indicates that household price measures lower average inflation during the energy crisis and narrow the gap between the lowest and highest income quintiles. Relief on household energy prices accounts for most of the narrowing, while motor-fuel measures partly offset it. These accounting effects depend on the reconstructed and calibrated price wedges and do not measure the policies' full welfare or macroeconomic effects.

This work can be extended in several directions. Transaction data could identify differences in prices paid and substitution within product categories, while linked income and balance-sheet data would permit a broader assessment of welfare effects, including the redistribution between debtors and creditors.

\section{Country Exposure to Extra-EU Import-Price Shocks}
\label{sec:app-import-cost-impulses}

For shock family $k$, let $h_k$ denote the observed change in the annual-average extra-EU27 import
unit-value index. The energy shock is 83.9 percent and the food shock is 21.0 percent between 2021
and 2022. These are common external-price innovations: country heterogeneity enters through import
exposure rather than through different values of $h_k$.

For destination country $d$ and industry $j$, the raw import-price shock becomes the imported-input
cost impulse
\begin{equation}
b_{dj}^{k}
=h_k\frac{\sum_{o\notin EU27}\sum_{i\in\mathcal S_k}Z_{oi,dj}}{x_{dj}},
\label{eq:appendix-import-cost-impulse}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal S_k$ is the set of shocked products, $Z_{oi,dj}$ is the intermediate-input flow from
origin $o$ and product $i$ to industry $j$ in country $d$, and $x_{dj}$ is that industry's gross
output. Thus, an identical raw price shock produces different cost impulses across countries and
industries because their extra-EU imported-input shares differ.

The output-weighted country impulse reported in
Table~\ref{tab:extra-eu-import-cost-impulses-country-2022} is
\begin{equation}
\bar b_d^k
=h_k\frac{\sum_j\sum_{o\notin EU27}\sum_{i\in\mathcal S_k}Z_{oi,dj}}
{\sum_j x_{dj}}.
\end{equation}
The euro-area row applies this ratio to EA20 sums; it is not a simple average of the country rows.

\section{The Household Budget Survey in Italy}
\label{sec:app-italy-hbs}

The most recent complete harmonized Eurostat HBS entry for Italy is 2005. We therefore combine it
with the 2015 and 2020 Istat public-use HBS files to construct the household-group baskets.

Age-group baskets are computed directly from the survey-weighted expenditure aggregates in the
Istat files, using the age of the household reference person.

For income quintiles, households are ranked by equivalised total consumption using the Carbonaro
scale and the survey weights. Because income quintiles are not observed in the target-year files, we
transport the relationship between income- and expenditure-quintile baskets observed in 2005. Let
$E_{jqy}$ denote expenditure on COICOP group $j$ by expenditure quintile $q$ in year $y$, and let
$s^{I}_{jq,2005}$ and $s^{E}_{jq,2005}$ denote the corresponding 2005 shares for income and
expenditure quintiles. The estimated income-quintile consumption structure is

\begin{equation}
\widehat{s}^{I}_{jqy}
=
\frac{E_{jqy}\left(s^{I}_{jq,2005}/s^{E}_{jq,2005}\right)}
{\sum_k E_{kqy}\left(s^{I}_{kq,2005}/s^{E}_{kq,2005}\right)}.
\label{eq:italy-income-expenditure-bridge}
\end{equation}

Thus, the 2005 income-to-expenditure ratio adjusts each 2015 or 2020 expenditure-quintile component,
after which the basket is renormalized to one. A few broad groups are combined to reconcile the two
2005 classifications. The resulting income-quintile structures are estimated rather than directly
observed. The procedure addresses the same
absence of jointly observed income and consumption discussed by \citet{donatiello2022joint}.

Table~\ref{tab:it-hbs-all-products} reports the resulting shares of total consumption. Age shares are
direct survey estimates; income-quintile shares are bridge estimates. The first and fifth income
quintiles account for about 8 and 39 percent of Italian consumption, respectively.

\section{RAS Weighting}
\label{sec:app-weight-normalization}

We use the RAS (Row and Column Adjustment Scaling) algorithm. RAS is a bi-proportional matrix
scaling method that alternately rescales rows and columns until the required marginal totals are
matched \citep{bacharach1965estimating}. In our setting, we combine official HICP item weights with
the relative HBS expenditure profile of each household group.

The baseline removes actual and imputed rents (HBS 041 and 042 and corresponding HICP
rent components) before constructing matched product baskets. Parent housing aggregates
are recalculated without these components. RAS matches group expenditure margins and annual
HICP product margins normalized over the remaining matched non-rent products.
The resulting indices therefore measure inflation excluding rents; agreement with the
all-items HICP is not an identity implied by this calibration.

Rent treatment can affect both the magnitude and the sign of income gaps because housing
tenure and rent shares vary across groups \citep{geerolf2023inflation}.
Table~\ref{tab:rent-mapping-robustness} retains three alternative conventions as a separate
sensitivity comparison: all rents maps HBS 041+042 to HICP 041; effective rents retains
HBS 041 only; excluding rents removes 041 and 042 and renormalizes the remaining basket.
This reference exercise uses relative-expenditure weights. It is distinct from the
RAS-based baseline and the RAS micro--macro comparisons in Appendix~\ref{app:aggregation-bias};
its excluding-rents column need not reproduce their estimates.

\input{tables/tab_EA20_rent_mapping_robustness_2021_06_2023_06}


We distinguish three objects: the HICP product shares, the aggregate product-by-group expenditure
mass calibrated by RAS, and the resulting within-group baskets. First, let $s_{g,y}$ denote the
share of group $g$ in total consumption expenditure used for HICP weight year $y$:
\begin{equation}
s_{g,y}
=
\frac{E^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{g,y'}}{E^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{\mathrm{all},y'}},
\qquad
\sum_g s_{g,y}=1,
\end{equation}
where $y'$ is the HBS wave matched to HICP weight year $y$. In the baseline, $y'=2020$ and the expenditure margins are computed from the same household sample as the group baskets. Historical applications select the most recent available preceding HBS wave, or the earliest wave when no preceding observation exists. These targets are expenditure shares, not population shares. For Italy, the income targets use the Istat-based HBS
inputs described in Appendix~\ref{sec:app-italy-hbs}.

Let $\mathcal{J}_y$ be the set of matched COICOP categories available in the HICP weighting
structure in year $y$. Let $E^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{j,g,y'}$ denote expenditure on item $j$ by group $g$ in the
matched HBS wave $y'$, and let $E^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{j,\mathrm{all},y'}$ denote expenditure on that item by all
households. Consistently with the implementation, the HBS correction factor used in the RAS seed is
the share of aggregate expenditure on item $j$ accounted for by group $g$:
\begin{equation}
c^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{j,g,y'}
=
\frac{E^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{j,g,y'}}{E^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{j,\mathrm{all},y'}}.
\end{equation}
Thus $c^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{j,g,y'}$ records how expenditure on item $j$ is distributed across household groups.
To avoid mixing index-weight units with expenditure shares, define the HICP weight normalized over
the matched product set as
\begin{equation}
\bar{\omega}^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{j,y}
=
\frac{\omega^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{j,y}}
{\sum_{k \in \mathcal{J}_y}\omega^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{k,y}},
\qquad
\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_y}\bar{\omega}^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{j,y}=1.
\end{equation}
The initial product-by-group mass is then
\begin{equation}
Z_{j,g,y}
=
\max\left\{
s_{g,y}\bar{\omega}^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{j,y}c^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{j,g,y'},\epsilon
\right\},
\qquad
\epsilon=10^{-12}.
\end{equation}
The small positive floor is a numerical safeguard to keep the RAS problem strictly positive
after HICP-HBS matching. RAS transforms this seed into a joint expenditure-share matrix
$M_{j,g,y}$. Its group margins equal the groups' expenditure shares, while its product margins equal
the normalized HICP shares:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_y} M_{j,g,y}
=
s_{g,y}
\quad \text{for all } g,
\qquad
\sum_g M_{j,g,y}
=
\bar{\omega}^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{j,y}
\quad \text{for all } j.
\end{equation}
Both sets of margins sum to one, so the constraints are mutually compatible.

The RAS algorithm starts from $M^{(0)}_{j,g,y}=Z_{j,g,y}$ and repeats two scaling steps. At
iteration $t$, the group-margin adjustment is
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{M}^{(t)}_{j,g,y}
=
M^{(t-1)}_{j,g,y}
\times
\frac{
s_{g,y}
}{
\sum_{\ell \in \mathcal{J}_y} M^{(t-1)}_{\ell,g,y}
},
\end{equation}
and the product-margin adjustment is
\begin{equation}
M^{(t)}_{j,g,y}
=
\widetilde{M}^{(t)}_{j,g,y}
\times
\frac{
\bar{\omega}^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{j,y}
}{
\sum_h \widetilde{M}^{(t)}_{j,h,y}
}.
\end{equation}
We iterate these two steps by weight year until the maximum absolute row or column marginal error is
below $10^{-10}$, with a maximum of 1,000 iterations.

The calibrated matrix $M$ is a joint expenditure distribution. The group-specific HICP basket is
the corresponding conditional product distribution within group $g$:
\begin{equation}
\omega_{j,g,y}
=
\frac{M_{j,g,y}}{s_{g,y}}
=
\frac{M_{j,g,y}}{\sum_{k \in \mathcal{J}_y}M_{k,g,y}}.
\end{equation}
The equality of the two denominators follows directly from the group-margin constraint. It implies
that each group basket sums to one. Moreover, the expenditure-share-weighted average of the group
baskets reproduces the normalized HICP product weights:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_y}\omega_{j,g,y}=1,
\qquad
\sum_g s_{g,y}\omega_{j,g,y}
=
\bar{\omega}^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{j,y}.
\end{equation}

The package implements the same system on a percentage scale. Internally, the calibrated mass is
$M^{\mathrm{pct}}_{j,g,y}=100M_{j,g,y}$, so its margins are $100s_{g,y}$ and
$100\bar{\omega}^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{j,y}$. The package returns
these percentage weights in the R variable \texttt{weighted\_consumption}. Denoting this variable
by $w^{\mathrm{code}}_{j,g,y}$, we have
\begin{equation}
w^{\mathrm{code}}_{j,g,y}
=
\frac{M^{\mathrm{pct}}_{j,g,y}}{s_{g,y}}
=
100\omega_{j,g,y},
\end{equation}
This quantity therefore sums to 100 within every household group. Thus division by $s_{g,y}$ in the code is the
percentage-scale counterpart of dividing $M_{j,g,y}$ by its group-row sum in the normalized
mathematical representation.

This preserves the relative information contained in the HBS expenditure profiles while making the
aggregate of the household-group baskets consistent with the published HICP weighting structure, up
to residual differences induced by product coverage and COICOP matching.

over the period beginning in 2019. The RAS

This pattern contrasts with the larger long-run gaps documented for the United States in
\cite{jaravel2024distributional}. Several methodological differences may account for the contrasting results. First, the
euro-area indices reported here aggregate national indices and therefore average across countries
whose price dynamics and fiscal responses can offset one another. Second, the euro-area calculation
uses harmonized COICOP categories, whereas the U.S. evidence exploits much finer product-level
data. Aggregation within broad categories can conceal persistent differences in the prices paid by
different income groups, which are captured in the U.S. data.
