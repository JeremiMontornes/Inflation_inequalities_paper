\begin{abstract}
We document inflation inequality in the euro area during the recent energy crisis using harmonized
HICP prices and household budget surveys. We construct household-level and group indices and counterfactual price paths excluding
household price policies. In 2022, the annual-average individual P20--P80 range reaches 4.8 percentage
points, compared with a 1.2-point gap between mean inflation in the first and fifth income quintiles.
Differences between household groups therefore capture only part of inflation inequality.
Fiscal policies designed to alter consumer prices can redistribute measured inflation exposure
across fixed household baskets. Counterfactual indices indicate that tariff shields, VAT cuts, fuel
rebates, and price caps reduced annual-average inflation by 1.7 points, the individual P20--P80
range by 1.8 points, and the Q1--Q5 gap by 0.6 point in 2022.

\vspace{1em}

\noindent \textbf{Keywords:} Inflation, Distribution of Income, Fiscal Policy\\
\noindent \textbf{JEL codes:} E31, D30, C43

\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

This paper documents how the recent inflation surge affected euro-area households. Inflation
rates in the euro area rose sharply from mid-2021, displaying considerable dispersion
across countries and households. The 2021--2023 episode was
a large relative-price shock, initially driven by imported energy and commodity prices.

%incidence therefore mainly depended on household exposure to energy, including heating and

%The consumer price index (CPI) typically reflects an average rate of price change for an average household. However, households consume different baskets of goods, leading to wide variations around the average. The main objective of this paper is to document how households face heterogeneous inflation because of the differences in their consumption patterns, and to measure the effects of fiscal policies decided in 2022-2023.


Inflation inequality emerges when relative-price movements interact with heterogeneous household
expenditure shares. If all prices rose proportionally, fixed-basket inflation would be identical
across households. We examine how recent energy-price shocks and the fiscal response shaped household inflation
exposure across the euro area. We construct monthly price indices for individual households and for groups defined by income, age, household size, and residence in each euro-area country. The indices combine 2020 HBS expenditure records with HICP prices following Eurostat's methodology. We then remove the main household price policies of 2021--2023 from component price paths. This accounting counterfactual holds expenditure weights fixed and excludes behavioral responses; policy effects are defined as observed minus counterfactual inflation.

Our main result is that household-level inflation dispersion rises sharply during the shock and is
substantially wider than differences between household groups. In 2022, the annual individual
P20--P80 range reaches 4.8 percentage points, while the annual Q1--Q5 gap reaches 1.2 points. A
model saturated with all interactions among income, age, household size, and residence explains
only 9.1\% of within-country variance on average across months, ranging from 5.1\% to 13.7\%.
The remaining 90.9\% lies within observationally similar household groups. Income quintiles thus
capture one systematic dimension of heterogeneity rather than the full distribution of household
outcomes.

The Q1--Q5 inflation gap nevertheless widens during the shock and varies across euro-area countries.
In 2022, it exceeds 3 points in Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, and the Netherlands and reaches 2.9
points in Ireland. Older and rural households also experience higher inflation during the rise in
energy prices. Household price policies reduce annual-average inflation by 1.7 percentage points,
the individual P20--P80 range by 1.8 points, and the Q1--Q5 gap by 0.6 point in 2022.

The literature measures household inflation by combining price changes with differences in
expenditure patterns. For the United States, \citet{hobijn2005inflation} reweight aggregate price indices for demographic groups. More recently, \citet{jaravel2024distributional} constructs long-run distributional
consumer price indices for the United States, closely following the BLS methodology. Using household-level expenditure or scanner data,
\citet{kaplan2017inflation,argente2021cost} document household-level dispersion within broad income and demographic groups.
Using barcode-level grocery purchases in the United States, \citet{oflaherty2026lookwithin} shows that demographic group averages mask most household-level inflation dispersion.
In the euro area, \citet{ampudia2024shopping} show how household
shopping behavior shapes the effect of monetary policy on inflation heterogeneity along the income distribution.
\citet{pallotti2024bears} combine consumption, income, and balance-sheet data to
measure welfare losses across euro-area households. Building on \citet{charalampakis2022impact},
we take a complementary approach to \citet{oflaherty2026lookwithin}. Depending on the exercise, we
cover 19 to 20 euro-area countries and the full consumption basket. We map harmonized HBS expenditure
categories to national HICP series, calibrate household baskets to official item weights using RAS,
and reconstruct policy counterfactuals at finer product detail. This design supports harmonized
cross-country comparisons but does not capture differences in prices paid, product quality, or
within-category substitution \citep{sangani2026cheapflation}.

A related literature evaluates the fiscal response to the recent inflation surge. Using household microsimulation models,
 \citet{pallotti2024bears} and \citet{amores2025inflation} show that fiscal interventions reduced welfare losses and inequality,
although broad price policies were less targeted than income support. Using detailed UK household data,
\citet{levell2026welfare} highlight a trade-off between targeting and efficiency: energy price subsidies
better protect households with high exposure to energy-price shocks, including within income groups, but distort consumption choices,
while transfers preserve price signals but may be less well targeted.
For Germany, \citet{krebs2024pricecontrols} examine conditions under which energy price controls
can be optimal, while \citet{dertwinkelkalt2025energy} show that the design of the energy price brakes
can induce suppliers to raise prices while reinforcing households' incentives to save energy. \citet{bonnet2025compensating} show, for France, that income-targeted transfers are more effective than fuel price subsidies at limiting welfare losses relative to household income.
We instead combine harmonized household inflation measures with monthly counterfactual price paths reconstructed at the
COICOP-component level for all 20 euro-area countries. This approach identifies which products drove the distributional
effect of household price policies: lower electricity and gas prices reduce the Quintile 1--Quintile 5 inflation gap,
while fuel policies partly offset that reduction because higher-income households spend more on private transport.

\section{Data and Methodology}

\label{sec:data-methodology}

Our approach has three core steps. We match HICP product-price indices to HBS expenditure records,
calibrate the resulting household and household-group baskets to official HICP product weights, and
aggregate the corresponding price indices across countries. This section focuses on those choices
and on the statistical objects used in the results. The appendices provide the full RAS algorithm,
national data exceptions, alternative income definitions, rent conventions, and validation tests.

\subsection{Sources, Samples, and Product Coverage}

\subsubsection*{Sources and Samples}

The analysis combines public HICP data with restricted Household Budget Survey (HBS) microdata and
national HBS inputs. Headline HICP inflation measures price changes for the aggregate household
consumption basket. Each national HICP uses price changes for goods and services weighted by
household expenditure from national accounts, household budget surveys, and other national sources.
Eurostat then aggregates the national indices with country weights that reflect each country's share
of euro-area household consumption expenditure.\footnote{The calculation follows Regulation (EU)
2016/792 and common data-collection practices. HICP products follow the European Classification of
Individual Consumption according to Purpose (ECOICOP), and historical series are mapped to the
classification used in the HBS. National statistical institutes compile the HICP in collaboration
with Eurostat.}

Eurostat updates the HICP product weights annually. The new weights enter with the January release
and remain fixed within the calendar year. Let $\mathcal J_y^{\mathrm{HICP}}$ denote the set of product categories
with official HICP weights in year $y$, $p_{j,y,m}$ the price index for product $j$ in month $m$, and
$p_{j,y,0}$ its price reference for the weight year, corresponding to the previous December. The
official within-year Laspeyres link and chained index are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
P_{y,m}
&=\sum_{j\in\mathcal J_y^{\mathrm{HICP}}}\omega^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{j,y}
\frac{p_{j,y,m}}{p_{j,y,0}},
&\qquad I_{y,m}&=I_{y-1,12}P_{y,m}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Figure~\ref{fig:hicp} shows inflation across the main HICP components in the euro area since January
2021. Energy and fuel prices rise sharply from mid-2021 and peak in 2022. Inflation subsequently
declines across most components, with energy prices falling more sharply than food prices and other
components.

Our primary expenditure source is the 2020 HBS microdata. The records contain expenditure by product, total net
household income, and household characteristics. Group-level indicators cover all 20
euro-area countries through the national and tabulation-based exceptions described below. Household
distributions cover 178,268 households in 19 countries; only Portugal is excluded because its
individual expenditure divisions cannot separate rents. Italy enters these household-level
calculations without an income filter. These households remain fixed across price
years, so the monthly series does not represent a panel of newly observed consumption choices.
Figure~\ref{fig:hbs_monthly_inflation_percentiles} follows this sample from January 2021 to December
2025. Analyses that jointly use income, age, household size, and degree of urbanization require
non-missing values for all four characteristics and therefore use a complete-case sample of 152,440
households.

The 2020 HBS wave combines surveys collected over country-specific reference periods. Fieldwork dates
and survey frequency therefore need not coincide across countries. The fixed wave provides a
cross-section of consumption heterogeneity rather than annual observations on changing household
baskets.\footnote{See \citet{accardo2023measuring} on estimating changes in consumption inequality
between survey waves.} Repeated Spanish HBS waves suggest that broad differences in expenditure
shares between low- and high-income households remain relatively stable even when aggregate
consumption patterns change (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:app-es-income-coicop-weights}).

\subsubsection*{Product Coverage and Treatment of Rents}

All HBS-based calculations in the main text use the three-digit ECOICOP1 classification. Depending
on the country, this level represents roughly 45--50 product groups in the source HBS. Source price
series must be complete over the full calculation period before aggregation, which keeps the product
composition within each three-digit category fixed over time. We reconstruct price interventions
at four-digit detail, but aggregate these price paths to the same
three-digit support before they are combined with HBS baskets.
Historical and validation exercises retain their explicitly stated matching levels.
Appendix~\ref{app:aggregation-bias} compares the individual P20--P80 inflation range across two- to
five-digit COICOP levels and reports the matching level used in each specification.

The baseline excludes actual and imputed rents and removes the corresponding HICP components before
matching. Parent categories are then rebuilt, and the remaining HBS expenditure and HICP product
weights are normalized over the common non-rent support. This coverage applies to household indices,
group indices, and policy counterfactuals. The resulting measures capture non-rent consumption-price
exposure rather than an all-items cost-of-living index. Table~\ref{tab:rent-mapping-robustness}
reports alternative rent conventions, while Appendices~\ref{sec:app-weight-normalization}
and~\ref{sec:app-validation-tests} report the corresponding normalization and validation exercises.

\subsubsection*{Household Groups and National Exceptions}

We construct groups by income, age of the household reference person, area of residence, and household size. Residence groups use
the Degree of Urbanisation (DEGURBA).\footnote{DEGURBA uses a common European methodology based on a
1 km$^2$ population grid. It distinguishes cities (densely populated areas), towns and suburbs
(intermediate-density areas), and rural areas (thinly populated areas). Urban centers have at least
50,000 inhabitants and 1,500 inhabitants per km$^2$; urban clusters have at least 5,000 inhabitants
 and 300 inhabitants per km$^2$; the remaining areas are rural.} For the two-group residence
comparisons, we combine towns and suburbs with rural areas and refer to this combined category as the
rural group; cities form the other group.

We use HBS survey weights throughout the construction of nationally representative group baskets.
Income groups rank households by total net household income without adjustment for household size,
so they describe household income rather than equivalized living standards. National
income-quintile thresholds are defined separately within each national HBS sample and survey wave. Missing income excludes a household from income
baskets but not from age, residence, or unconditional analyses when the required variables are
available. Appendix~\ref{sec:app-income-definition} motivates this choice, relates it to
\citet{klick2024equivalized}, and compares it with equivalized income while holding household
inflation rates, the sample, and weights fixed.

The main 2021--2023 analysis uses fixed 2020 HBS baskets. For each national group, we aggregate
individual product expenditure into a representative consumption basket. Three national exceptions
preserve broader group coverage. Italy uses the national group
baskets described in Appendix~\ref{sec:app-italy-hbs}. Portugal uses published group tabulations for
non-rent inflation because its individual expenditure divisions cannot isolate rents. Malta uses
published residence-area baskets and relative-expenditure weighting because the required groups and
RAS margins are unavailable.

\subsection{Calibration and Price Indices}
\label{sec:household-inflation-rates}

\subsubsection*{RAS Calibration}

Unadjusted HBS expenditure shares do not generally aggregate to the annual HICP product weights. We
therefore apply Row and Column Adjustment Scaling (RAS) to the household--product expenditure matrix.
The calibration preserves relative HBS expenditure profiles while matching two margins: each
household's share of retained national expenditure and each product's normalized HICP weight.
Normalizing the calibrated cells within each household yields household-specific product weights.
For household groups, we apply the same procedure after replacing household margins with each
group's share of total expenditure. In both cases, the expenditure-weighted average of the calibrated
baskets reproduces the official product shares on the matched non-rent support. Appendix~\ref{sec:app-weight-normalization}
provides the full algorithm, notation, convergence rule.

Figure~\ref{fig:hbs-basket-share-heterogeneity} summarizes heterogeneity in household baskets before
conditioning on realized inflation. For each broad product category, we compute the survey-weighted
P20, median, and P80 of household budget shares within each country and then average each percentile
across countries. The horizontal segment reports P20--P80 and the point marks the median.

\subsubsection*{Household and Group Price Indices}

Let $p_{c,j,y,m}$ denote the product price index in month $m$ of year $y$, and let
$\mathcal J_{c,y}^{\mathrm M}$ denote the matched non-rent product set for country $c$. Product indices are first
expressed relative to the preceding December, aggregated with annual household weights, and then
chained to the preceding December's household index:\footnote{See Eurostat's \texttt{hicp} R
package, which implements the Eurostat HICP aggregation.}
\begin{equation}
P_{ic,y,m}
=\sum_{j\in\mathcal J_{c,y}^{\mathrm M}}\omega_{j,ic,y}
\frac{p_{c,j,y,m}}{p_{c,j,y-1,12}},
\qquad
I_{ic,y,m}=I_{ic,y-1,12}P_{ic,y,m}.
\label{eq:household-within-year-price-index}
\end{equation}

The household application uses fixed 2020 expenditure profiles, while annual HICP margins update
aggregate product composition. Group indices use the same expression after replacing household
weights $\omega_{j,ic,y}$ with RAS-calibrated group weights $\omega_{j,g,c,y}$ obtained by aggregating
HBS expenditures before calibration; they therefore need not equal averages of separately calibrated
household indices. The baseline uses the 2020 HBS wave throughout, while historical exercises using
successive waves retain their stated matching convention.

\subsection{National and Euro-Area Aggregation}

We use two aggregation procedures for two distinct objects. First, the distributional bands in
Figure~\ref{fig:hbs_monthly_inflation_percentiles} summarize national household-inflation
distributions. Within each country, we calculate the mean and the 10th, 20th, 80th,
and 90th empirical percentiles of household year-on-year inflation. We then average each national
statistic using Eurostat's annual HICP country expenditure weights. For a national statistic
$s_{c,y,m}$, the reported series is
\begin{equation}
s_{y,m}=\sum_c\omega_{c,y}s_{c,y,m},
\qquad
\omega_{c,y}=\frac{W_{c,y}}{\sum_{k\in\mathcal C}W_{k,y}},
\label{eq:national-statistic-aggregation}
\end{equation}
where $W_{c,y}$ is the published all-items HICP country weight and $\mathcal C$ is the fixed country
sample. These bands average national percentile endpoints; they are not percentiles of a pooled
euro-area distribution. The country weights are not additionally adjusted for rents.

Second, aggregate household and group price indices require aggregation of December-relative price
links before chaining. Let $q_{c,y}$ denote the annual HICP country expenditure weight after removing
rent expenditure and normalizing over the included countries, so $\sum_c q_{c,y}=1$. The household
aggregate is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
P_{c,y,m}
&=\frac{\sum_i a_{ic}E_{ic,y}P_{ic,y,m}}{\sum_i a_{ic}E_{ic,y}},\\
P_{y,m}&=\sum_c q_{c,y}P_{c,y,m},
\qquad I_{y,m}=I_{y-1,12}P_{y,m}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:euro-area-chain-aggregation}
\end{equation}
The same sequence applies to group indices using group-specific expenditure masses. Averaging
household inflation rates directly does not implement this annual chain aggregation. All baseline inflation
results exclude actual and imputed rents. Official headline series and the
explicitly identified rent-sensitivity and validation exercises retain their stated comparison
coverage.
Figure~\ref{fig:fr-validation-level} reconciles the household mean and the chained micro aggregate
with the matching Eurostat non-rent benchmark for the same 19-country sample.

\section{Main Results}
\label{sec:main-results}

This section first establishes the magnitude of individual household inflation dispersion using the
P20--P80 range, then asks how much of that dispersion observable characteristics jointly explain.
It next turns from this variance accounting to economically interpretable shifts in distributions and
group means across income, age, residence, and household-size groups. The final two subsections
decompose individual dispersion and the income-group inflation gap by product and document how both
measures differ across countries during the 2021--2023 energy crisis.

\subsection{Cross-Country Heterogeneity During the 2022--2023 Energy Shock}


Cross-country differences reflect variation in energy mixes, household
consumption patterns, and fiscal responses. These factors shaped how the energy-price shock, which
began in 2021 and intensified, especially in natural gas markets, after Russia's invasion of
Ukraine, translated into household inflation.

Table~\ref{tab:country-heterogeneity-cumulative-gap} documents this cross-country heterogeneity by
reporting the individual P20--P80 inflation range and the annual Quintile 1--Quintile 5 inflation gap.
Individual dispersion peaks in 2022 in most countries and is especially large in Estonia (10.7 points),
Lithuania (10.1), Belgium (8.0), Italy (7.7), and Latvia (7.6), compared with 4.8 points for the
19-country aggregate.
The Q1--Q5 gap is
largest in 2022 in most countries: it exceeds 3 points in Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, and the Netherlands and reaches
2.9 points in Ireland. Several countries subsequently record a negative gap in 2023 as energy-price
pressures recede, while the euro-area gap falls from 1.2 points in 2022 to zero in 2023. This
cross-country pattern in the Q1--Q5 inflation gap is qualitatively similar to
\citet{claeys2022inflation}, who also find that low-to-high-income inflation gaps are larger in
countries with higher aggregate inflation.

The same interaction between price dynamics and expenditure shares operates across countries. A
price increase in a product category generates inflation inequality only to
the extent that the affected category has different expenditure weights across household groups.
Large aggregate shocks may generate limited inequality when price increases fall on categories with
similar weights across quintiles.

\section{Contributions of Household Price Policies to Inflation Inequality}
\label{sec:fiscal-policies}


This section isolates the direct contribution of household price policies to average inflation and
inflation inequality. We first describe the interventions and their fiscal scale, then construct
monthly price paths without the policies and estimate their euro-area and country-level effects.

\subsection{Household Price Policies during the Energy Crisis}
\label{sec:energy-price-policies}

During the 2021--2023 energy crisis, euro-area governments used electricity and gas price caps, VAT
and energy-tax reductions, fuel rebates, and support applied directly to household energy bills
\citep{arregui2022targeted,dao2023unconventional,garciaMiralles2023support}. These interventions
change the prices paid by households and therefore enter household price indices directly. The inventory primarily covers
energy measures, together with temporary food-price policies.

We combine the Bruegel energy-policy dataset \citep{sgaravatti2021national} with national sources and
organize the measures into four instrument groups: VAT and non-fuel excise reductions; administered
prices; subsidies, rebates, and other fuel-price reductions; and other household-bill measures.
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:ea20-price-counterfactual-measures} reports the country-level inventory.

Figure~\ref{fig:fiscal-cost-instrument} summarizes the scale and composition of the response. The
documented gross fiscal cost is EUR~220.1 billion, at least 1.6 percent of euro-area GDP, of which
EUR~118.1 billion (53.7\%) is associated with untargeted measures. This predominance of broad-based
price support is consistent with the Bruegel and OECD inventories
\citep{sgaravatti2021national,hemmerle2023aiming}. Administered-price policies account for the largest
instrument share. Fiscal cost measures the scale of the intervention, not its household incidence or
the size of the price counterfactual.

\subsection{Constructing Counterfactual Price Indices}
\label{sec:no-policy-construction}

We compare observed, policy-inclusive retail prices with counterfactual price paths that remove the
covered household price policies. This comparison holds consumption quantities fixed and allows only
prices to differ between the ``with-policy'' and ``without-policy'' scenarios. It is therefore an
accounting counterfactual rather than a behavioral one: it does not capture quantity adjustments, such
as the observed decline in residential energy consumption during the crisis. For each instrument, we
use the available national evidence, including statutory rules, published reference paths,
administrative data, or comparable-country ratios.

Counterfactual component price paths are first constructed at the four-digit COICOP level by replacing
each observed HICP component index with its counterfactual counterpart; they are then aggregated to
the common HBS support described in Section~\ref{sec:data-methodology}. Let $p_{j,t}$
denote the observed HICP component index for product $j$ in month $t$, and let $\widetilde p_{j,t}$
denote the corresponding counterfactual index. Here $t$ indexes calendar months, corresponding to
the year--month pair $(y,m)$ used above. Let $r_{j,t}$ denote the observed retail price per unit
and $\widetilde r_{j,t}$ its counterfactual counterpart, expressed in the same monetary units.
For each simulated policy, we apply the implied price ratio to the observed
component index\footnote{When several policies apply to the same product in the same country and month, the R scripts combine their counterfactual-to-observed price ratios by adding their deviations from one to a baseline ratio of one.}:
\begin{equation}
\widetilde p_{j,t}
=
p_{j,t}
\frac{\widetilde r_{j,t}}{r_{j,t}} .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{VAT and Non-Fuel Excise-Tax Reductions.}
For VAT reductions, the observed retail price includes the temporary tax rate. We recover the counterfactual price by removing that rate and applying the standard rate:
\begin{equation}
\widetilde r_{j,t}
=
r_{j,t}
\frac{1+\tau^{0}_{j,t}}{1+\tau^{1}_{j,t}},
\end{equation}
where $\tau^{0}_{j,t}$ is the standard VAT rate and $\tau^{1}_{j,t}$ is the temporary rate. Non-fuel ad valorem excises follow the same ratio logic. The statutory rate changes are available for all VAT measures in the inventory---Belgium, Croatia, Cyprus, Finland, Germany, Ireland, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Portugal, and Spain;
when a measure covers only part of an HICP component or a subset of households,
we adjust the statutory ratio for the documented coverage rather than imputing a
missing tax rate. For Dutch energy-tax relief, published with-policy bills $B_k^{1}$ and statutory
support amounts $S_k$ imply $\widehat{\rho}_k=1+S_k/B_k^{1}$.

\paragraph{Administered Prices.}
For regulated-price shields and caps, we use an external reference path where available, such as a
commodity-price benchmark or a tariff published by the national regulator. If
$T^{\mathrm{obs}}_{j,t}$ denotes the regulated tariff actually applied and
$T^{\mathrm{ref}}_{j,t}$ the tariff that would have applied without the intervention, the
counterfactual retail price is:
\begin{equation}
\widetilde r_{j,t}
=r^{\mathrm{ref}}_{j,t}
=r_{j,t}\frac{T^{\mathrm{ref}}_{j,t}}{T^{\mathrm{obs}}_{j,t}} .
\end{equation}
Country-specific reference paths or policy wedges are available for Austria, Germany, the Netherlands,
Italy, Spain, France, Portugal, and Slovenia (Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:tariff-shield-formula-comparison}). France uses published no-shield tariff paths;
Austria applies its statutory electricity-brake formula to monthly E-Control prices; and Italy derives
a period-specific aggregate wedge from ARERA social-bonus funding and domestic energy expenditure.
The remaining country-specific ratios use official program rules or administrative settlement data.
For Dutch electricity and gas price caps, published bill and support information determines the
counterfactual ratio. When both bills are reported, as for district heating, we use
$\widehat{\rho}_k=B_k^{0}/B_k^{1}$, where $B_k^{0}$ is the no-policy bill.

\paragraph{Subsidies, Rebates, and Other Fuel-Price Reductions.}
For unit subsidies and fuel rebates, we add the statutory absolute price wedge to the observed
monthly price path or, without a retail unit-price series, calibrate it in HICP component-index
units. Evidence from
\citet{drolsbach2023pass} suggests that temporary fuel-tax reductions and discounts were largely
passed through to consumer prices in 2022 in several euro-area countries, although pass-through
varied over time and by fuel type. This supports treating these policies as retail-price wedges in
the counterfactual construction.
Under full pass-through, adding the statutory support amount $s_{j,t}$ to the observed unit price gives:
\begin{equation}
\widetilde r_{j,t}
=
r_{j,t}+s_{j,t}.
\end{equation}
For transport-fuel rebates, $s_{j,t}$ is recovered from the statutory pump discount, expressed in
euros per liter, and applied to a monthly retail-fuel price $r^{\mathrm{M}}_{j,t}$, where the upright
superscript $\mathrm{M}$ denotes the observed market price per liter corresponding to HICP component
$j$. Following \citet{bonnet2025compensating}, we refer to these interventions as pump-price
rebates or subsidies because they reduced the tax-inclusive price paid by consumers, although their
technical implementation sometimes operated through fuel excise taxation rather than through a
direct transfer to households. The no-rebate fuel index is obtained by rescaling the observed HICP
transport-fuel component:
\[
\widetilde p_{j,t}
= p_{j,t}
\frac{r^{\mathrm{M}}_{j,t}+s_{j,t}}{r^{\mathrm{M}}_{j,t}} .
\]
These transport-fuel policies are applied only to fuels for personal transport
equipment. The product decompositions nevertheless
report the broader motor-fuels-and-vehicle-operation category; its remaining components retain their observed price paths
unless they are affected by a separately identified policy.

The inventory identifies statutory unit rebates or excise changes for motor fuels in Belgium,
Cyprus, Estonia, France, Germany, Greece, Ireland, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Portugal,
and Spain, and for heating fuels in Cyprus and Slovenia. We use these source values whenever the
retail-price denominator and policy coverage allow a direct conversion into a counterfactual ratio.
The reconstructed ratio, $\widehat{\rho}_k$, is the unit price without the policy divided by the
price paid under the policy. If the source provides an effective unit support $s_k$ and a
representative with-policy unit price $r_k^{1}$, then
$\widehat{\rho}_k=1+s_k/r_k^{1}$; if it instead provides the no-policy unit price $r_k^0$, then
$\widehat{\rho}_k=r_k^0/(r_k^0-s_k)$. We adjust $s_k$ for documented coverage or pass-through.

When the source does not provide a usable unit amount or rate, price denominator, or coverage
weights, we use the comparable-policy imputation described below.

\paragraph{Other Household-Bill Measures.}
This residual category contains household-bill interventions that do not fit the three preceding
economic categories: VAT and non-fuel excise reductions, administered prices, or fuel-price
reductions. We construct their counterfactual ratios from the source-based bill or support information
available for each measure.

\paragraph{Comparable-Policy Imputation.}
When a direct reconstruction lacks a required input, irrespective of the instrument category, we
estimate a counterfactual-to-observed price ratio from comparable euro-area policies. For measure
$k$ in recipient country $c$, $\bar{\rho}^{\mathrm D}_{k,-c}$ denotes the mean source-based ratio
among policies matched by product group and retail-price mechanism, excluding country $c$. We set
$\widehat{\rho}_k=\bar{\rho}^{\mathrm D}_{k,-c}$, first averaging multiple measures within each
donor country and then weighting donor countries equally. We record these imputations separately
from direct reconstructions. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:ea20-policy-method-sensitivity} compares the
full specification with direct reconstructions only and with alternative conditional-benchmark
wedges. For the incomplete-input measures in Cyprus, Ireland, and Latvia, the Netherlands is the
sole donor.

We then replace observed component indices with these counterfactual paths and apply the same
RAS-calibrated weights and household-group, national, and euro-area aggregation procedures as in
Section~\ref{sec:data-methodology}.

\subsection{Euro-Area Effects on Inflation and Inflation Inequality}
\label{sec:temporal-fiscal-effects}

Household price policies reduced euro-area annual inflation by 0.1 percentage point in 2021, 1.7
points in 2022, and 0.6 point in 2023. Counterfactual inflation exceeds observed inflation during
the main phase of the energy shock, with the largest difference around the late-2022 peak
(Figure~\ref{fig:ea_policy_total_cpi_counterfactual}). The 2022 estimate is
consistent in magnitude with \citet{dao2023unconventional}, who find that such fiscal policies
reduced euro-area inflation by 1--2 percentage points in 2022 under different
modeling assumptions.

A reduction in the price level need not lower inflation in every subsequent year. When a temporary policy expires or its price discount shrinks, observed prices rise relative to the lower comparison base created by the policy. Observed annual inflation can then exceed counterfactual inflation even if the observed price level remains no higher than its no-policy counterpart. Positive annual policy effects therefore can reflect the withdrawal of earlier support and base effects, rather than a higher price level caused by the policy.

sign. In 2023, its policies reduce average inflation by 0.5 point but increase the individual

These estimates measure basket exposure to national component-price paths, not household
eligibility or behavioral responses. Fiscal costs also refer to different budget periods and may
combine forecast and realized amounts. Inflation-gap effects are
percentage-point changes, not monetary household gains.
Cross-country variation reflects the interaction between each policy's product coverage and household
expenditure shares. Policies concentrated on products with dispersed expenditure shares have larger
effects on the P20--P80 and Q1--Q5 gaps; policies covering similarly weighted products mainly shift
average inflation. Fiscal cost measures budgetary scale rather than the size or distribution of the
price wedge, so it does not predict the magnitude of the inflation-gap effect.

\section{Inflation Burden}
\label{sec:inflation-burden}

Our indicators measure differences in inflation exposure across household groups, but they do
not directly express the cost of inflation relative to household resources. We therefore construct
the burden from HBS microdata rather than combining group-level inflation with an external aggregate
consumption-to-income ratio. For household $i$ in country $c$, let $X_{ic}$ denote consumption
expenditure on the non-rent products matched to HICP, $Y_{ic}$ net household income, and
$\bar{\pi}_{ic,y}$ the household's average annual inflation rate in year $y$. The raw
household-level burden is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{raw}}_{ic,y}=\bar{\pi}_{ic,y}\frac{X_{ic}}{Y_{ic}},
\label{eq:micro-inflation-burden}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{\pi}_{ic,y}$ is measured in percent, so $\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{raw}}_{ic,y}$ is measured
in points of net income. The indicator allows both individual inflation exposure and the
consumption-to-income ratio to vary across households. The expenditure numerator and inflation
index cover the same matched non-rent products. This fixed-resource exposure indicator is not
compensating variation: it holds 2020 expenditure and income fixed and allows neither substitution
nor income adjustment.

Very small reported incomes can generate mechanically extreme expenditure-to-income ratios. We
therefore winsorize $100X_{ic}/Y_{ic}$ at the survey-weighted 99th percentile within each country,
while retaining the underlying household inflation rate. Denoting the resulting ratio by
$\widetilde{\kappa}_{ic}$, the burden used in the estimates and its aggregation for national income
quintile $q$ are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{B}_{ic,y}
&=\bar{\pi}_{ic,y}\frac{\widetilde{\kappa}_{ic}}{100},\\
\mathcal{B}_{q,c,y}
&=\frac{\sum_{i\in q(c)}a_{ic}\mathcal{B}_{ic,y}}
{\sum_{i\in q(c)}a_{ic}},\\
\mathcal{B}_{q,y}
&=\sum_{c\in\mathcal C_{\mathcal B}}
\omega^{\mathcal B}_{c,y}\mathcal{B}_{q,c,y}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:aggregate-micro-inflation-burden}
\end{equation}
where $a_{ic}$ is the HBS survey weight, $q(c)$ indicates that total-net-income quintiles are
defined separately within each country, and $\mathcal C_{\mathcal B}$ is the 18-country burden
sample. The survey weights determine each national quintile mean. The annual HICP country
expenditure weights $\omega^{\mathcal B}_{c,y}$ then aggregate these national means after being
normalized to sum to one over $\mathcal C_{\mathcal B}$, as for the other distributional statistics
in Section~1.3.
The 2020 HBS basket, income, and expenditure observations are held fixed, while household-specific
inflation is calculated for 2021--2023. For each household-year, the inflation rate is computed over
the matched non-rent expenditure categories and the HBS--HICP product weights are renormalized to
one. We impose no minimum matched-expenditure threshold.

Net household income enters this calculation twice: it determines the national income quintile and
appears in the denominator of $X_{ic}/Y_{ic}$. The Q1--Q5 gradient can therefore arise partly from
the mechanical expenditure-to-income gradient, even if households face the same inflation rate.
We report the estimates as a descriptive fixed-resource exposure measure and do not interpret the
quintile difference as the causal effect of heterogeneous inflation. Separating the inflation and
expenditure-to-income components requires a common-inflation counterfactual and sensitivity checks
using medians, alternative winsorization thresholds, and equivalized income.

Figure~\ref{fig:ea20-inflation-burden} uses household indices and matched non-rent expenditure.
The first quintile faces a burden of 2.5 percent of net income in 2021, 8.6 percent in 2022, and
4.8 percent in 2023, compared with 1.5, 4.6, and 3.0 percent for the fifth quintile. The Q1--Q5 gap
is 1.0, 3.9, and 1.8 points, respectively, calculated before rounding. The income and expenditure
filters retain positive-income households; national quintiles are assigned before these
burden-specific exclusions.

\section{Conclusion}


%This paper a set of public indicators to measure inflation inequalities in real-time monthly
%across socio-demographic groups in the Euro Area.

%Inflation inequality during the 2021--2023 episode varied more across countries, age groups, and
%residence areas than across income quintiles in the euro-area aggregate.

This paper constructs household-specific inflation indices to measure inflation inequality in the
euro area. Its central result is that dispersion across individual households is substantially wider
than differences between broad household categories. At the inflation peak in October 2022, the
P20--P80 range reached 6.5 percentage points, nearly three times the 2.2-point difference between the
mean inflation rates of the first and fifth income quintiles. A model saturated with interactions
among income, age, household size, and urbanization explains only 9.1\% of household
inflation variance on average; 90.9\% remains within observationally similar groups. The annual
product decomposition reinforces this result: motor fuels and vehicle-operation expenditure
accounts for 2.8 points of the 4.8-point P20--P80 range in 2022, while housing energy accounts for
another 2.3 points. Detailed differences in heating and
mobility needs therefore generate substantial inflation heterogeneity that group averages cannot
fully capture \citep{oflaherty2026grocery}.

Group indicators nevertheless identify systematic exposure gradients. The annual Q1--Q5 inflation
gap peaked at 1.2 points in 2022, while monthly gaps reached 2.2 points for income, 1.8 points for age,
and 1.3 points between rural and city households during the energy shock. Housing energy widened
the Q1--Q5 gap because it carries greater budget weight among lower-income households, whereas motor
fuels partly narrowed that gap because private-transport expenditure is higher among richer
households. The same product can thus have different implications for individual dispersion and for
differences between group means.

Household price policies cushioned both dimensions of inflation inequality. In 2022, they reduced
annual-average inflation by 1.7 percentage points, the individual P20--P80 range by 1.8 points, and
the Q1--Q5 gap by 0.6 point. The corresponding reductions in 2023 were 0.6, 0.7, and 0.6 point.
Housing-energy relief accounts for most of the narrowing, while policies affecting motor fuels
and vehicle operation partly offset the reduction in individual dispersion. These fixed-basket accounting
effects depend on the products covered, the timing and size of the price wedge, and household
expenditure shares.

The inflation burden provides a complementary perspective by scaling household inflation by matched
non-rent expenditure relative to net income. In 2022, this burden reached 8.6\% of net income for the
first income quintile and 4.6\% for the fifth, a gap of 3.9 points. This gradient combines differences
in inflation exposure with the higher expenditure-to-income ratios of low-income households. It is
therefore a descriptive fixed-resource measure rather than a welfare effect attributable solely to
heterogeneous inflation.

Linking household-specific price indices to tax-benefit and balance-sheet data would extend the
analysis to income indexation, transfers, debt, and wealth, allowing a broader assessment of
purchasing-power changes and policy incidence.

\section{RAS Weighting}
\label{sec:app-weight-normalization}

We use the RAS (Row and Column Adjustment Scaling) algorithm. RAS is a bi-proportional matrix
scaling method that alternately rescales rows and columns until the required marginal totals are
matched \citep{bacharach1965estimating}. In our setting, we combine official HICP item weights with
the relative HBS expenditure profile of each household group.

We distinguish three objects: the HICP product shares, the aggregate product-by-group expenditure
mass calibrated by RAS, and the resulting within-group baskets. First, let $s_{g,y}$ denote the
share of group $g$ in total consumption expenditure used for HICP weight year $y$:
\begin{equation}
s_{g,y}
=
\frac{E^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{g,y'}}{E^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{\mathrm{all},y'}},
\qquad
\sum_g s_{g,y}=1,
\end{equation}
where $y'$ is the HBS wave matched to HICP weight year $y$. In the baseline, $y'=2020$ and the expenditure margins are computed from the same household sample as the group baskets. Historical applications select the most recent available preceding HBS wave, or the earliest wave when no preceding observation exists. These targets are expenditure shares, not population shares. For Italy, the income targets use the Istat-based HBS
inputs described in Appendix~\ref{sec:app-italy-hbs}.

Let $\mathcal{J}_y^{\mathrm M}$ be the set of matched COICOP categories available in the HICP weighting
structure in year $y$. Let $E^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{j,g,y'}$ denote expenditure on item $j$ by group $g$ in the
matched HBS wave $y'$, and let $E^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{j,\mathrm{all},y'}$ denote expenditure on that item by all
households. Consistent with the implementation, the HBS correction factor used in the RAS seed is
the share of aggregate expenditure on item $j$ accounted for by group $g$:
\begin{equation}
c^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{j,g,y'}
=
\frac{E^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{j,g,y'}}{E^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{j,\mathrm{all},y'}}.
\end{equation}
Thus $c^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{j,g,y'}$ records how expenditure on item $j$ is distributed across household groups.
To avoid mixing index-weight units with expenditure shares, define the HICP weight normalized over
the matched product set as
\begin{equation}
\bar{\omega}^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{j,y}
=
\frac{\omega^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{j,y}}
{\sum_{k \in \mathcal{J}_y^{\mathrm M}}\omega^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{k,y}},
\qquad
\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_y^{\mathrm M}}\bar{\omega}^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{j,y}=1.
\end{equation}
The initial product-by-group mass is then
\begin{equation}
Z_{j,g,y}
=
\max\left\{
s_{g,y}\bar{\omega}^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{j,y}c^{\mathrm{HBS}}_{j,g,y'},\epsilon
\right\},
\qquad
\epsilon=10^{-12}.
\end{equation}
The small positive floor is a numerical safeguard to keep the RAS problem strictly positive
after HICP-HBS matching. RAS transforms this seed into a joint expenditure-share matrix
$M_{j,g,y}$. Its group margins equal the groups' expenditure shares, while its product margins equal
the normalized HICP shares:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_y^{\mathrm M}} M_{j,g,y}
=
s_{g,y}
\quad \text{for all } g,
\qquad
\sum_g M_{j,g,y}
=
\bar{\omega}^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{j,y}
\quad \text{for all } j.
\end{equation}
Both sets of margins sum to one, so the constraints are mutually compatible.

The RAS algorithm starts from $M^{(0)}_{j,g,y}=Z_{j,g,y}$ and repeats two scaling steps. At
iteration $t$, the group-margin adjustment is
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{M}^{(t)}_{j,g,y}
=
M^{(t-1)}_{j,g,y}
\times
\frac{
s_{g,y}
}{
\sum_{\ell \in \mathcal{J}_y^{\mathrm M}} M^{(t-1)}_{\ell,g,y}
},
\end{equation}
and the product-margin adjustment is
\begin{equation}
M^{(t)}_{j,g,y}
=
\widetilde{M}^{(t)}_{j,g,y}
\times
\frac{
\bar{\omega}^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{j,y}
}{
\sum_h \widetilde{M}^{(t)}_{j,h,y}
}.
\end{equation}
We iterate these two steps by weight year until the maximum absolute row or column marginal error is
below $10^{-10}$, with a maximum of 1,000 iterations.

The calibrated matrix $M$ is a joint expenditure distribution. The group-specific HICP basket is
the corresponding conditional product distribution within group $g$:
\begin{equation}
\omega_{j,g,y}
=
\frac{M_{j,g,y}}{s_{g,y}}
=
\frac{M_{j,g,y}}{\sum_{k \in \mathcal{J}_y^{\mathrm M}}M_{k,g,y}}.
\end{equation}
The equality of the two denominators follows directly from the group-margin constraint. It implies
that each group basket sums to one. Moreover, the expenditure-share-weighted average of the group
baskets reproduces the normalized HICP product weights:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_y^{\mathrm M}}\omega_{j,g,y}=1,
\qquad
\sum_g s_{g,y}\omega_{j,g,y}
=
\bar{\omega}^{\mathrm{HICP}}_{j,y}.
\end{equation}

When reported as percentages, the group-specific weights are $100\omega_{j,g,y}$ and therefore sum
to 100 within every household group. This rescaling leaves the RAS constraints and the resulting
price indices unchanged.

This preserves the relative information contained in the HBS expenditure profiles while making the
aggregate of the household-group baskets consistent with the published HICP weighting structure, up
to residual differences induced by product coverage and COICOP matching.

\section{Income-Group Baskets in Italy}
\label{sec:app-italy-hbs}

\begingroup
\footnotesize
This appendix explains the construction of Italy's income-group indices. The Italian files provide
the three-digit expenditure vectors needed for household-level indices, so Italy enters the individual
P20--P80 distributions without an income filter. They do not provide comparable income jointly with
those household expenditure vectors. Italy therefore remains excluded from household-level
saturated-cell regressions, while its income-group indices use the bridge below.

For income, we combine the 2005 harmonized income-quintile profile with the 2015 and 2020 Istat
files. Households are ranked by Carbonaro-equivalized consumption using survey weights. Let $E_{jqy}$
be expenditure on bridge group $j$ by expenditure quintile $q$, $s^{I}_{jq,2005}$ and
$s^{E}_{jq,2005}$ the 2005 income- and expenditure-quintile shares, and $n_{jy}$ the national share.
The script transfers the 2005 relationship and contracts the gradient toward the national margin:

\begin{equation}
\widetilde{s}^{I}_{jqy}
=
\frac{E_{jqy}\left(s^{I}_{jq,2005}/s^{E}_{jq,2005}\right)}
{\sum_k E_{kqy}\left(s^{I}_{kq,2005}/s^{E}_{kq,2005}\right)},
\qquad
\widehat{s}^{I}_{jqy}
=
\frac{n_{jy}+\lambda_j\left(\widetilde{s}^{I}_{jqy}-n_{jy}\right)}
{\sum_k\left[n_{ky}+\lambda_k\left(\widetilde{s}^{I}_{kqy}-n_{ky}\right)\right]}.
\label{eq:italy-income-expenditure-bridge}
\end{equation}

Here $\lambda_j$ is 0.20 for housing, 0.35 for transport and recreation, and 0.65 otherwise.
The division targets are allocated to three-digit products using national within-division shares and
RAS-calibrated to HICP weights. They are group-level bridge estimates, not household observations
\citep{donatiello2022joint}. Table~\ref{tab:it-hbs-all-products} reports the RAS group margins.

This pattern contrasts with the larger long-run gaps documented for the United States by
\citet{jaravel2024distributional}. Several methodological differences may account for the contrasting results. First, the
euro-area indices reported here aggregate national indices and therefore average across countries
whose price dynamics and fiscal responses can offset one another. Second, the euro-area calculation
uses harmonized COICOP categories, whereas the U.S. evidence exploits much finer product-level
data. Aggregation within broad categories can conceal persistent differences in the prices paid by
different income groups, which are captured in the U.S. data.
